# Climate, atmosphere and ocean

Climate, weather, atmospheric and ocean models.

Each data set below has a short summary of the science: what the model computes, what varies, and what the active subspace shows. The numbers come from the data set's notebook, which has the details and the comparison with the paper. The [top-level README](../README.md#data-sets) has the paper links and the kind of each data set (stored data or Python model).

### [Arctic_Snow_Depth_SMRT](Arctic_Snow_Depth_SMRT/)

Yan et al. ask how well snow depth on Arctic sea ice can be retrieved from passive microwave observations. The Snow Microwave Radiative Transfer model SMRT computes the brightness temperatures T_B of one dry snow layer on first-year sea ice from five snow inputs (depth, density, temperature, grain radius and stickiness), here at 400 Monte Carlo samples with finite-difference gradients. For T_B at 36.5 GHz V the first eigenvalue holds 77.9 % of the trace (λ₁/λ₂ = 7.54) and the first two 88.2 %; the first direction mixes stickiness, grain radius, density and depth, and a cubic in two active variables has leave-one-out R² 0.857 (0.927 without the 41 samples above 458.35 kg m⁻³, where the paper's dense-snow correction makes T_B jump). The gradient ratio GR(36/18) has practically the same subspace, while the 6.9 and 10.7 GHz H channels are one-dimensional in density (λ₁/λ₂ = 2151 and 455). Sobol' indices estimated from the same data give the same leading parameter as the paper's EFAST results in all 12 channels.

### [Atmospheric_Selenium_Lifetime](Atmospheric_Selenium_Lifetime/)

Feinberg et al. built the first global atmospheric selenium model in the aerosol-chemistry-climate model SOCOL-AER and ran it 400 times on a Latin hypercube over 34 uncertain inputs of the selenium cycle: rate constants, photolysis, emission totals and speciation, aerosol uptake and a "dummy" aerosol. The output is the global selenium lifetime τ, burden divided by deposition, whose median over the runs (4.36 d) matches the paper's 4.4 d. With no gradients, a linear fit and a ridge-penalized quadratic give the same first direction (|w₁ᵀŵ₁| = 0.986), led by the OCSe + OH rate constant k₁₃, which also leads the paper's Sobol' ranking. A quadratic in two active variables predicts τ with leave-one-out R² 0.501, as well as the penalized quadratic in all 34 inputs (0.492) and better than one variable (0.428), so the notebook takes two dimensions. About half the variance of τ is not predicted out of sample, partly because 8 runs in its long upper tail hold 0.435 of its sum of squares, and λ₂/λ₃ (1.88) shows no gap after the second eigenvalue.

### [FaIR_Climate_Response_Calibration](FaIR_Climate_Response_Calibration/)

FaIR is an emission-driven simple climate model, and fair-calibrate v1.4.1 constrains 45 of its parameters to an 841-member posterior that reproduces historical warming and assessed climate metrics. The notebook runs that posterior under SSP2-4.5 with finite-difference gradients and studies the 2081–2100 warming and the equilibrium climate sensitivity (ECS). For the warming the gradient-based subspace has λ₁/λ₂ = 4.9 and needs four dimensions before a quadratic reaches a 10-fold cross-validated R² of 0.917, while a quadratic in the single least-squares direction (mostly the feedback κ₁ and the present-day aerosol–cloud forcing) also reaches 0.917 and one in w₁ only 0.635: the warming is close to a one-dimensional ridge that the leading eigenvector does not find. ECS is nearly one-dimensional (λ₁/λ₂ = 89.7) and w₁ finds it. On this multimodal, heavy-tailed posterior the gradient matrix is dominated by large local gradients near the switch-on of the aerosol–cloud shape parameters, and with the aerosol–cloud scale β as an input instead of the 2005–2014 forcing, the leading direction explains none of the warming.

### [Hector_Matilda_Warming](Hector_Matilda_Warming/)

Hector is an open-source simple carbon-cycle climate model, and Brown et al.'s Matilda package draws its six uncertain parameters from literature priors, runs the model as a perturbed parameter ensemble and weights the members against observed temperature. The notebook runs Hector 3.1.1 under SSP2-4.5 at 1000 Monte Carlo draws from Matilda's priors, with finite-difference gradients at 400 of them. The 2081–2100 warming is essentially a function of one direction (λ₁/λ₂ = 36.8, roughly 28–47 under a ±30 % change of the step-sensitive NPP₀ gradient component; λ₁ holds 95.8 % of the trace), dominated by climate sensitivity with smaller carbon-cycle and aerosol contributions. A quadratic in that one variable predicts the warming with leave-one-out R² 0.983, and CO₂ in 2100 has a weaker one-dimensional structure built from the carbon-cycle parameters. The notebook also recomputes the paper's weighted probability of warming between 1.7 and 3.8 °C: 96.9 %, against the paper's 92 %.

### [Ice_Sheet_Basal_Melt_Ensemble](Ice_Sheet_Basal_Melt_Ensemble/)

Berdahl et al. ran the Community Ice Sheet Model (CISM v2.1) on the whole Antarctic ice sheet at 4 km for 200 years, 500 times, each run forced by its own sigmoid basal-melt anomaly in each of five ocean basins, with three parameters per basin (melt anomaly at year 200, inflection time and time scale; 15 inputs). The output is the sea-level contribution at simulation year 200 from the 491 runs that reached it. With no gradients, a global quadratic fit gives two gaps (λ₁/λ₂ = 6.97, λ₂/λ₃ = 3.74) and two active directions: the first led by the Amundsen-basin melt rate and timing with a smaller East Antarctic share, the second a contrast of the shape and size of the Amundsen sigmoid. A quadratic in the first active variable has leave-one-out R² 0.900, two raise it to 0.951, close to the 0.972 of a full quadratic in all 15 inputs. At year 100 the subspace is one-dimensional (λ₁/λ₂ = 11.77).

### [Lorenz96_Initial_State_Inference](Lorenz96_Initial_State_Inference/)

Schuster, Constantine and Sullivan infer the 36 initial values and the forcing F of the chaotic Lorenz-96 system (m = 37) from noisy observations of a trajectory, and report a four-dimensional active subspace from an importance-weighted posterior covariance. The notebook regenerates the setting in numpy, with exact gradients of the data misfit, and is mainly a cautionary case. On the window [0, 1] the gradient matrix gives a clean one-dimensional subspace (λ₁/λ₂ = 84.3, quadratic leave-one-out R² 0.941), but it is almost the F axis, because the true F = 8 lies four prior standard deviations from the prior mean, and a quadratic in F alone already has leave-one-out R² 0.922. On the paper's window [0, 10] two of the 1000 samples carry 99.6 % of the trace and the gradient estimate fails (leave-one-out R² 0.004), although derivative-free estimates from the same samples recover the F direction. The paper's posterior-covariance subspace cannot be reproduced: the importance weights collapse onto one sample, and the Laplace posterior covariance has no gap after its fourth eigenvalue (λ₄/λ₅ = 1.03 on [0, 1], 1.06 on [0, 10]).

### [Marine_Carbonate_System_PyCO2SYS](Marine_Carbonate_System_PyCO2SYS/)

PyCO2SYS solves the marine carbonate system: from total alkalinity A_T and dissolved inorganic carbon T_C, with temperature, salinity, pressure and nutrients, it computes pH, the partial pressure of CO₂ (pCO₂), the carbonate-mineral saturation states and the rest of the system. The notebook treats 14 inputs as independent Gaussians (six measured inputs, seven equilibrium constants and total borate) and computes active subspaces of pCO₂, pH and the two saturation states from 1000 samples with finite-difference gradients. Over measurement uncertainty at the paper's test conditions the model is nearly linear and pCO₂ has a one-dimensional subspace (λ₁/λ₂ = 1.10×10⁴), led by the second dissociation constant of carbonic acid. In a surface-ocean study with a wide spread of A_T, T_C, temperature and salinity, the first gap (λ₁/λ₂ = 176.4) is the T_C − A_T contrast with temperature, but the larger relative gap is after λ₂ (λ₂/λ₃ = 1398, mostly temperature), so a two-dimensional subspace is the better-separated one.

### [Parcel_Droplet_Activation](Parcel_Droplet_Activation/)

An adiabatic cloud-parcel model predicts the maximum supersaturation S_max of a rising air parcel, and so the number of activated droplets, from eight aerosol and thermodynamic inputs; Rothenberg and Wang emulate it with polynomial chaos and rank the inputs by Sobol' indices. The notebook recomputes the study with the parcel model pyrcel on 300 Latin-hypercube points, with exact (automatic-differentiation) gradients of log₁₀ S_max. On the paper's input box restricted to μ_g ≥ 3.16 nm and κ ≥ 0.1 (240 of the 300 points), the active subspace is one-dimensional (λ₁/λ₂ = 29.5; a cubic in the active variable has leave-one-out R² 0.981): S_max rises with updraft speed and falls with aerosol number and size, with the paper's first three inputs in its order (size, updraft, number). On the full box the estimate breaks down (λ₁/λ₂ = 1.61), because the derivative in the hygroscopicity κ grows like 1/κ as κ → 0.
