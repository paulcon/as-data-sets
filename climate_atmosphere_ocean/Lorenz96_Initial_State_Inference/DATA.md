# Lorenz96_Initial_State_Inference: data

Kind: **Python model**. The model is `lorenz96_model.py` (numpy only), and `generate_data.py` writes every
data file from it with two saved seeds. The setting is Sec. 5.2 of an arXiv preprint (no journal version
found); its ground-truth initial state and noise realization are not reported, so the ones here are our own,
drawn from a stated seed.

## Files

| file | contents | role |
|---|---|---|
| `Lorenz96_Initial_State_Inference.ipynb` | the case study (Python 3, active_subspaces v1) | executed top to bottom with `SEED = 293`; outputs committed |
| `lorenz96_model.py` | `rhs`, `trajectory`, `synthetic_data`, `misfit_and_grad`, `misfit`, `misfit_complex_step`, `leading_lyapunov`; constants `K`, `DT`, `NOISE_VAR`, `PRIOR_VAR`, `F_TRUE`, `NAMES` | the model |
| `generate_data.py` | writes the five CSVs; `--rows a:b --out DIR --check` recomputes a subset and compares | regenerates the data |
| `check_accuracy.py` | recomputes the numbers of this file that the notebook and `generate_data.py` do not print | checks |
| `truth.csv` | 1 × 37: the synthetic ground truth (X_1(0), …, X_36(0), F) | data-generating input |
| `observations.csv` | 1001 × 37: t, then d_1 … d_36 at t = 0, 0.01, …, 10 | synthetic observations |
| `inputs.csv` | 1000 × 37 prior draws, columns in the order of `NAMES` (X1_0 … X36_0, F) | model input |
| `outputs.csv` | 1000 × 7: Φ_T for T = 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10 | model output |
| `gradients.csv` | 1000 × 259: column 37 w + j is ∂Φ_T/∂x_j for the w-th window, **physical** inputs | model output |

Every CSV has two `#` lines (a description and the column names) and is read by
`np.loadtxt(path, delimiter=',')`. Values are written with 17 significant digits, so they round-trip exactly.
All quantities are dimensionless (the Lorenz-96 state, forcing and time have no units). Total size 6.62 MB (6.32 MiB; `check_accuracy.py` check 4).

## Model

**Paper.** I. Schuster, P. G. Constantine and T. J. Sullivan, "Exact active subspace Metropolis–Hastings,
with applications to the Lorenz-96 system", arXiv:1712.02749v1 [stat.CO], 7 December 2017,
doi:10.48550/arXiv.1712.02749. Preprint; no journal version was found. All page references are to it.

**Equations.** The one-level Lorenz-96 system on a ring of K = 36 variables,
dX_k/dt = (X_{k+1} − X_{k−2}) X_{k−1} − X_k + F (indices mod 36). The paper (Sec. 5.2, p. 8) prints the
two-scale system, whose X equation is −X_{k−1}(X_{k−2} − X_{k+1}) − X_k + F − (hc/b) Σ_j Y_j, with fast
variables Y_j; it says it "picked a system in 36 dimensions", infers only initial values and F, and gives no
values for h, c, b, J or for the Y_j initial values. We read this as the one-level system with K = 36
(the two-scale coupling term dropped). Open question below.

**Inputs** (m = 37):

| name | symbol | prior | truth used for the data | source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `X1_0` … `X36_0` | X_1(0) … X_36(0) | N(0, 4) each, independent | one draw from the prior, `default_rng(293)` (`truth.csv`) | prior: Sec. 5.2, p. 8; truth: not reported |
| `F` | F | N(0, 4) | 8 | Sec. 5.2, p. 8 |

The paper: "We defined a prior density over all inference variables (initial values and the forcing constant)
as an isotropic Gaussian centered at 0 with variance 4 in every dimension" (p. 8). The notebook's input space
is `ac.Gaussian(0, 4 I)`, so every input is normalized by the same standard deviation, 2.

**Observations.** "Data d was generated by computing a forward solve from the ground truth using a BDF method
with a timestep of 0.01 between time 0 and 10 and adding independent centred Gaussian noise with variance 0.1
to the all points generated by the forward solve" (p. 8). Here: the truth trajectory at t_i = 0.01 i,
i = 0 … 1000 (t = 0 included; the paper does not say), all 36 components, plus N(0, 0.1) noise. Draw order
(`generate_data.truth_and_data`): `rng = default_rng(293)`; X_true(0) = 2·`rng.standard_normal(36)`;
noise = √0.1·`rng.standard_normal((1001, 36))`.

**Quantity of interest.** The data misfit (negative Gaussian log-likelihood up to a constant, p. 8) over
the window [0, T]: Φ_T(x) = Σ_{t_i ≤ T} Σ_k (X_k(t_i; x) − d_ik)² / (2 · 0.1), for
T = 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10. T = 10 is the paper's window; the notebook analyses T = 1 in detail (about
two e-folding times; not fixed by a rule stated in advance, and it has the largest λ1/λ2 of the seven windows,
so 84.3 is a selected value). This data set is mainly a cautionary case:
- On [0, 1] the gradient subspace is one-dimensional (λ1/λ2 = 84.3, quadratic LOO R² = 0.941), but it is the
  mean-gradient direction (|cos| = 1.000; the mean gradient carries 0.769 of trace(C)) and almost the F axis
  (|w1,F| = 0.988), because the true F = 8 lies four prior sd from the prior mean. A quadratic in F alone
  already has LOO R² = 0.922 and a linear model in all 37 inputs 0.915.
- On [0, 10] the gradient Monte Carlo estimate fails: gradient norms run from a median of 3.94e5 to 4.14e9,
  two of the 1000 samples carry 81.7 % and 17.9 % of trace(C) (99.6 % together), w1 and w2 are those two
  gradients, and a quadratic in w1ᵀx has LOO R² = 0.004. A subspace close to F still exists: unit gradients
  give |w1,F| = 0.9998, the least-squares slope |b_F|/|b| = 0.995, the global quadratic (qphd) λ1/λ2 = 13.95
  with |w1,F| = 0.988, and a quadratic in F alone LOO R² = 0.866.

At
the truth, Φ_1 = 1834.18 and Φ_10 = 18202.15, close to the noise-only expectation of half the number of
observations (1818 and 18018; `check_accuracy.py` check 1).

**Solver.** Classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta, fixed step Δt = 0.01 (`lorenz96_model._step`). The paper
used "a BDF method with a timestep of 0.01" and gives no order, tolerance or code. The data and the misfits
here come from the same discrete map, so the inference problem is self-consistent; it is not the paper's
trajectory (see Accuracy).

**Gradients.** Exact derivatives of the discrete map: the tangent-linear (forward-sensitivity) recursion of
the RK4 scheme, propagating dX/dx (36 × 37) with the state (`misfit_and_grad`). Gradients are with respect to
the physical inputs. The notebook passes them with the Gaussian space, which scales them by 2.

**Sampling design.** M = 1000 independent Monte Carlo draws from the prior N(0, 4 I_37),
`default_rng(1712).standard_normal((1000, 37))` × 2. The notebook uses the first 500 rows for the paper's posterior-covariance method (the paper's
"500 samples from the prior", p. 8). No input value lies outside physical meaning (all real values are
admissible); F ranges over −6.31 … 6.70. All 1000 rows are distinct; none equals the prior mean
(`check_accuracy.py` check 4).

**Published results.** Text only (p. 8): with 500 prior samples and the posterior-covariance method
(Sec. 2.1.2), "the spectral gap was found between the 4th and 5th largest eigenvalues, making the active
subspace 4-dimensional." No eigenvalues, eigenvectors or figure of them are given. Figs. 5.6–5.7 are MCMC
results (a KDE of the F–X_36 marginal; autocorrelations, eASMH improving short-lag autocorrelation "by a factor
of around 0.84"), which this data set does not reproduce. In this notebook, the paper's method applied to our
data collapses (effective sample size 1.000 on [0, 10] and on [0, 1]: the second-best of 500 samples has a
misfit larger by 22807 and 2257), so the weighted covariance is zero to machine precision. A tempered
likelihood with ESS = 50 gives a spectrum indistinguishable from noise (permuting the same weights over the
samples gives λ4/λ5 in 1.03–1.23, 5–95 %, against the actual 1.20), so no conclusion is drawn from it. The
Laplace (Gauss–Newton) posterior covariance at the truth, the matrix the paper's estimator targets for a
near-Gaussian posterior, has no gap after the 4th eigenvalue (λ4/λ5 = 1.03 on [0, 1], 1.06 on [0, 10]) and
no F in its leading directions (F components of eigenvectors 1–4 at most 0.108 and 0.000; posterior sd of F
1.9e-2 and 8.9e-4, prior sd 2). The paper's Fig. 5.6 (p. 9; read from the figure) shows an eASMH marginal of
F with its mode near 1, from about −6 to about 8, prior-like and centred far from the truth 8.

## Regenerating

```bash
python generate_data.py                                       # all files, written here
python generate_data.py --out <scratch dir> --check           # all files, compared with the files here
python generate_data.py --rows 0:20 --out <scratch dir> --check   # 20 samples only
```

`--check` and `--rows` refuse to run without `--out` pointing to another directory, so the shipped files are
never overwritten. Two seeds (`SEED_DATA = 293` for truth and noise, `SEED_SAMPLES = 1712` for the prior
draws) fix everything. The shipped files were written in a Linux container (2026-10-31). In `.venv`
(Python 3.11, numpy 2.4.6, macOS, 2026-11-02) a full `generate_data.py --out <scratch dir> --check` took 259 s
on one process and reproduced all five files byte for byte: the largest difference printed is 0.0e+00 for
every file. Other numpy versions or platforms may change the last digits of `outputs.csv` and
`gradients.csv` (rounding level, about 1e-16 relative). Memory stays below 100 MB (samples are processed in
batches of 250).

## Accuracy

- **Gradients.** The notebook's check cell compares the stored gradients at rows 0 and 1 with complex-step
  derivatives (step 1e-30, `misfit_complex_step`, an independent path through the forward code): largest
  relative error 2e-16 on [0, 1] and 5e-16 on [0, 10]; and with central differences of step 1e-6 in each
  physical input: 4e-9 on [0, 1] and 8e-9 on [0, 10].
- **Time stepping** (`check_accuracy.py` check 2). Against `scipy.integrate.solve_ivp` (DOP853,
  rtol = atol = 1e-13) from the truth, the RK4 trajectory differs by at most 1.32e-5 on [0, 1] and 5.93e-5 on
  [0, 2] (the truth state has standard deviation 3.58 over [0, 10]). The difference passes 1e-3 at t = 3.43 and 1 at t = 6.64: on the
  chaotic attractor (leading Lyapunov exponent 2.10 along the truth trajectory, printed in the notebook) any
  two solvers part ways after a few time units. So Φ_T for T ≥ 5 is a property of this discrete map, as the
  paper's was of its BDF solver; the misfit is consistent because data and model use the same map.
- **Failure** (`check_accuracy.py` checks 3 and 4). For initial values far outside the prior (alternating
  ±100, F = 8), RK4 at Δt = 0.01 is unstable; the model raises `FloatingPointError` instead of returning a
  value. Alternating ±50 still runs. No prior sample comes near this (largest |input| in `inputs.csv`: 9.24).

## How these files relate to the paper

- **Not the paper's computation.** The paper's code, truth, noise and samples are not published. These files
  are an independent numpy computation of the setting it describes, with: the one-level equations (the paper
  prints two-scale equations); RK4 instead of an unspecified BDF; t = 0 observed; our own truth initial state
  and noise (seed 293) and prior samples (seed 1712).
- **Different matrix.** The paper's active subspace is the posterior covariance from importance-weighted prior
  samples (Sec. 2.1.2); the files here support the gradient matrix of the misfit (Sec. 2.1.1, the
  construction of Constantine, Kent and Bui-Thanh 2016) and, from `outputs.csv` alone, the posterior
  covariance. The notebook computes both, plus the Laplace posterior covariance at the truth (from the model,
  by complex step) and derivative-free (least-squares and global-quadratic) subspaces.
- **Truth not on the attractor.** The truth's initial state is a prior draw (spread 1.90 over the 36
  components at t = 0), not a point on the attractor (spread 3.66, mean 2.46 over t in [5, 10]), so [0, 1]
  includes the truth's transient; a spun-up truth could change the short-window results.
- **Window.** The paper uses [0, 10] only. The extra windows are our addition, to show where gradient-based
  active subspaces work for this problem.

## Open questions (only the authors can settle them)

1. Were the equations the one-level Lorenz-96 system with K = 36? If two-scale, what were h, c, b, J and the
   fast-variable initial values?
2. What was the ground-truth initial state, and was it a prior draw or a point on the attractor?
3. Which BDF solver (code, order, tolerances), and does "all points generated by the forward solve" include t = 0?
4. What were the posterior-covariance eigenvalues and the effective sample size of the 500 importance weights?
   With noise variance 0.1 on 36 036 observations, we find one dominant weight (ESS 1.000), and the Laplace
   posterior covariance at the truth has no gap after the 4th eigenvalue, so we cannot see how a
   4-dimensional gap arose; were the weights tempered, normalized differently, or computed on a different
   likelihood?
5. Fig. 5.6 (p. 9) puts the eASMH posterior mode for F near 1, with mass from about −6 to about 8, a prior-like
   width, while under a Laplace approximation at our truth the posterior standard deviation of F is about
   1e-3 (8.9e-4 on [0, 10], printed in the notebook). Was the likelihood scaled (tempered,
   or a different noise variance), or did the chains not converge? Was the 4-D subspace built from degenerate
   weights?
