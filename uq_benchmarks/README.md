# UQ benchmark collections

Standard test problems for uncertainty quantification and dimension reduction methods.

Each data set below has a short summary of the science: what the model computes, what varies, and what the active subspace shows. The numbers come from the data set's notebook, which has the details and the comparison with the paper. The [top-level README](../README.md#data-sets) has the paper links and the kind of each data set (stored data or Python model).

### [Elliptic_PDE_Random_Coefficients](Elliptic_PDE_Random_Coefficients/)

Constantine, Dow and Wang build kriging surfaces on active subspaces and test them on an elliptic PDE, −∇·(a∇u) = 1 on the unit square, whose log-coefficient is a Karhunen–Loève series with 100 independent standard normal coefficients. The output is the average of the solution over the right boundary, computed with a finite-volume solver and adjoint gradients at 300 samples, for a long (β = 1) and a short (β = 0.01) correlation length. The eigenvalues drop sharply after the first (λ₁/λ₂ about 900 and about 160), so both fields have a one-dimensional active subspace; for the short correlation length the weight of the first eigenvector is spread over many more of the 100 inputs. A kriging surface on the single active variable has a mean relative error of 0.133 (β = 1) and 8.08×10⁻³ (β = 0.01) at 500 test points, and a second active variable does not help. The active-subspace surfaces beat surfaces on the most sensitive input coordinates in every case, and beat a full-space kriging surface of the same cost for the long correlation length, where the output is not nearly linear.

### [Fire_Satellite_Coupled_System](Fire_Satellite_Coupled_System/)

Sankararaman and Mahadevan propagate uncertainty through a conceptual design model of a forest-fire-monitoring satellite with three disciplines, orbit, attitude control and power, the last two coupled by feedback. The model has nine normal inputs and three outputs, the total torque, the total power and the solar array area; the coupled fixed point is solved for 2000 samples, with exact gradients by the complex step. The total power is almost a linear function of one direction (λ₁/λ₂ = 12162; a linear fit has leave-one-out R² 0.99992), almost entirely the non-attitude-control power P_other. The solar array area is also one-dimensional (λ₁/λ₂ = 4705), led by P_other and the solar flux (leave-one-out R² 0.99969 for a quadratic). The total torque depends on the drag coefficient and the aerodynamic moment arm through their product; its gaps are λ₁/λ₂ = 36.3 and λ₂/λ₃ = 72.3, and a second active variable raises the leave-one-out R² of a quadratic from 0.98313 to 0.99963.

### [Golinski_Speed_Reducer](Golinski_Speed_Reducer/)

Golinski posed the minimum-weight design of a two-gear speed reducer (gearbox); with the number of pinion teeth fixed at 17 it is a standard six-variable test problem for constrained optimization and dimension reduction. The notebook studies the gearbox volume and two constraints on the first shaft, its deflection and its stress, with exact gradients at 1000 uniform samples on the standard design box. The volume has a one-dimensional active subspace (λ₁/λ₂ = 246.1), led by the face width and the module and then the two shaft diameters, on which a linear function has leave-one-out R² 0.996. The deflection constraint is one-dimensional (λ₁/λ₂ = 1714), with weights on the shaft diameter, shaft length and module (leave-one-out R² 0.999516 for a cubic). The stress constraint is, to 1 − |w₁,d₁| = 2.9×10⁻⁶, a function of the shaft diameter d₁ alone (λ₁/λ₂ = 8.939×10⁶).

### [Hat_Stiffened_Panel_Shear_Buckling](Hat_Stiffened_Panel_Shear_Buckling/)

The model, from Ko and Jackson of NASA Dryden, is the critical shear load of the face-sheet strip between two hat stiffeners: a closed-form flat-plate buckling formula with a correction for the shear carried by the hat. It depends on ten geometric and material inputs with narrow uniform and truncated-normal distributions, two of which, the widths of the stiffener foot and hat, are dependent (Spearman correlation −0.8); the sheet is about 0.032 in thick, as in the memorandum's panel. With exact gradients at 5000 points, the load has a one-dimensional active subspace (λ₁/λ₂ = 1188, 99.895 % of the trace), led by the Young modulus (+0.885) and then the stiffener flank length, the thickness and the stiffener spacing. A line in the active variable has leave-one-out R² 0.999013, and Nataf-transformed variables give the same direction (|w₁·w₁′| = 0.9992). At the midpoint of the inputs the load is 1107 lb/in, 1.23 times the memorandum's finite-element value of 900 lb/in, which the memorandum says its closed-form prediction slightly exceeds.

### [OpenAeroStruct_Wing_Aerostructural](OpenAeroStruct_Wing_Aerostructural/)

OpenAeroStruct (Jasa, Hwang and Martins) couples a vortex-lattice aerodynamic model with a beam model of the wing spar and computes fuel burn from the Breguet range equation; Hokanson's psdr package made it a 12-input test function (twist, spar thickness, root chord, taper, angle of attack and three spar material constants). The data are 2000 Monte Carlo samples on psdr's design box with OpenMDAO total-derivative gradients. In 1195 of them the spar is over its allowable stress, and the analysis keeps the 805 wings within it, the paper's structural constraint. On these wings fuel burn has a one-dimensional active subspace (λ₁/λ₂ = 49.1, between 46.1 and 56.7 over four independent draws), set by the angle of attack, the root chord, the twist and the taper, the inputs that fix the lift coefficient and the wing area; the spar thicknesses and material constants hardly matter. A quadratic in the active variable has leave-one-out R² 0.966, and 0.994 with a second active variable. Over the whole box a few near-divergent samples dominate the gradients and give a two-dimensional subspace led by the root spar thickness, which changes from draw to draw.

### [PDE_Inverse_Problem_MCMC](PDE_Inverse_Problem_MCMC/)

Constantine, Kent and Bui-Thanh speed up Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) by running the chain on the active variables of the data misfit and drawing the inactive variables from the prior. The main example infers the 100 Karhunen–Loève coefficients of the log-coefficient of an elliptic PDE, with a standard Gaussian prior, from noisy observations of the solution at seven points next to the right boundary; the subspace comes from adjoint misfit gradients at 1000 prior samples. The misfit has gaps after the first and second eigenvalues (λ₁/λ₂ = 12.9, λ₂/λ₃ = 5.1), and the notebook uses two active variables, as the paper does; the misfit is most sensitive to the coefficient near the right boundary, where the observations are. The active-variable chains mix much better in the 100 inputs, because the 98 inactive directions are drawn independently, but with these data the two-dimensional approximation shifts the posterior means of the active variables by up to half a posterior standard deviation and misses what the data say about the third active variable. A two-parameter quadratic example, with eigenvalues more than four orders of magnitude apart, shows the method where the approximation error is small.

### [Point_Heat_Source_Consolidation](Point_Heat_Source_Consolidation/)

Booker and Savvidou derived a closed-form solution for the temperature, excess pore pressure and displacement around a constant point heat source, such as a canister of heat-emitting nuclear waste, buried deep in a saturated poro-thermo-elastic soil or rock. The notebook evaluates it with exact gradients at 1000 random draws of the eight uncertain material properties of Chaudhry et al. and studies the three quantities at r = 6 m, t = 1 year and on a 10 × 10 grid in (r, t). The temperature change has a one-dimensional active subspace led by the thermal conductivity (λ₁/λ₂ ≈ 118; leave-one-out R² 0.9969 for a cubic), and the pore pressure one led by the permeability (λ₁/λ₂ ≈ 52; 0.9675). The radial displacement needs two directions (ratios 9.3 and 21), mixing the permeability and the thermal expansion of the solid; a cubic in two active variables has leave-one-out R² 0.9805. Over the (r, t) grid the notebook maps the eigenvalue ratio and the leading input of each field value.

### [River_Flood_Dyke_Overflow](River_Flood_Dyke_Overflow/)

Lamboni et al. test derivative-based global sensitivity measures on a closed-form simplification of the 1-D Saint-Venant equations, which gives the maximal annual overflow of a river above a dyke. The overflow depends on eight independent inputs with truncated Gumbel, truncated normal, triangular and uniform distributions, and the notebook computes exact gradients at 10 000 points. With every input scaled by its standard deviation, the overflow has a one-dimensional active subspace (λ₁/λ₂ = 33.2, 96.0 % of the trace), led by the flowrate, the dyke height, the downstream level and the Strickler (friction) coefficient: the paper's four most important inputs by total Sobol' index, in the same order. A line in the active variable has leave-one-out R² 0.9777, and exact quadrature reproduces the paper's Sobol' indices to within 0.008.

### [Simply_Supported_Tube_Deflection](Simply_Supported_Tube_Deflection/)

A circular tube on simple supports carries a point force at some distance from the left support, and Euler–Bernoulli beam theory gives in closed form the deflection under the load and the slopes at the two supports. All six inputs, the force, the length, the load position, the outer and inner diameters and the Young's modulus, are random, with the independent normal, uniform and triangular distributions of the OpenTURNS use case. From 1000 Monte Carlo samples with exact gradients, each output has a two-dimensional active subspace (λ₂/λ₃ between 71 and 161), spanned almost exactly by the load position and an "amplitude" direction, the combination of force, outer diameter and Young's modulus in F/(EI). A quadratic in the two active variables reproduces the deflection with leave-one-out R² 0.9975. The data are the benchmark as posed rather than a prediction for a real tube: the tube is stubby (L/D_e ≈ 1.9), and Timoshenko theory puts the shear deflection at about 0.7 times the bending deflection.

### [Speed_Reducer_Shaft_Limit_State](Speed_Reducer_Shaft_Limit_State/)

The reliability example of Du and Sudjianto is the limit state of a shaft in a speed reducer: its strength minus the combined bending–torsion stress. It has five independent random inputs, the shaft diameter, the span, the external force (Gumbel), the torque and the strength (uniform), and the notebook uses 1000 Monte Carlo samples with exact gradients. The active subspace is one-dimensional with each input scaled by its standard deviation (λ₁/λ₂ = 94; leave-one-out R² 0.99354 for a quadratic) and with each input mapped through its CDF to a standard normal (λ₁/λ₂ = 15.71; 0.96335). In both, the torque carries the largest weight, then the force and the strength with opposite signs, while the diameter and the span, whose standard deviations are 0.1 mm, carry almost none. The notebook also estimates the failure probabilities of the article's Tables 9–11: its Monte Carlo values agree with the article's, while the article's FORM and SORM values are not reproduced.

### [Steel_Column_Reliability](Steel_Column_Reliability/)

In the second example of Kuschel and Rackwitz, the yield-stress limit state of a 7500 mm steel column depends on nine independent random inputs: the yield stress, three loads, three section dimensions, the initial deflection and the Young's modulus, with lognormal, normal, Gumbel and Weibull marginals. The notebook studies two designs, the widely used benchmark design and the paper's own optimum, with the inputs mapped through their CDFs to standard normal variables and exact gradients at 2000 Monte Carlo samples each. Both have a one-dimensional active subspace (λ₁/λ₂ = 88.99 and 88.10) close to the first-order reliability (FORM) direction, and a linear function of the active variable has leave-one-out R² 0.98772 and 0.98508. In the benchmark design the direction is led by the yield stress, then the flange thickness, the two variable loads and the initial deflection. Monte Carlo puts the failure probability of the paper's design at 1.24×10⁻³, 1.43 times the FORM value.

### [Sulfur_Aerosol_Radiative_Forcing](Sulfur_Aerosol_Radiative_Forcing/)

Charlson et al. estimate the global-mean direct shortwave forcing of anthropogenic sulfate aerosol with a box model that is a product of nine uncertain factors: sulfur source, sulfate yield and lifetime, scattering efficiency, hygroscopic growth, upscatter fraction, transmittance, cloud-free fraction and surface coalbedo; their nominal values give −1.3 W m⁻². The notebook gives the factors the independent lognormal distributions of the uncertainty-quantification benchmark version of the model (Penner et al.) and computes the active subspace from 1000 samples with exact gradients. With respect to the logarithms of the inputs the forcing is an exponential of a linear function, so the active subspace is exactly one-dimensional, with weights proportional to the log-scale standard deviations of the inputs; a cubic in the active variable has leave-one-out R² 0.9946. In the physical coordinates nearly the same first direction appears (|w₁ᵖʰʸˢ·w₁ˡᵒᵍ| = 0.9997), but with a smaller gap (λ₁/λ₂ = 25.2) and a worse one-dimensional fit (leave-one-out R² 0.8713).

### [Truss_23_Bar_Midspan_Deflection](Truss_23_Bar_Midspan_Deflection/)

A simply supported plane truss of 23 linear-elastic bars (span 24 m, height 2 m) carries six random vertical loads, and the Young's moduli and cross-section areas of its horizontal and oblique bars are random: ten independent lognormal and Gumbel inputs, as in the truss example of Blatman and Sudret. The output is the downward deflection at midspan, and the active subspace is computed in the paper's standard-normal coordinates from 1000 Monte Carlo samples with exact adjoint gradients. It is one-dimensional (λ₁/λ₂ = 105.49, 97.558 % of the trace), and a quadratic in the active variable has leave-one-out R² 0.9873. Stiffer or thicker bars reduce the deflection and larger loads increase it; the horizontal bars outweigh the oblique ones by a factor 5.5, and the loads nearest midspan weigh most. The normalized activity scores of this one direction agree with the paper's reference total Sobol' indices to within 0.0148.

### [Two_DOF_Equipment_Structure_Oscillator](Two_DOF_Equipment_Structure_Oscillator/)

Igusa and Der Kiureghian derive, by perturbation theory, a closed-form mean-square response of a light piece of equipment (a secondary oscillator) mounted on a structure (a primary oscillator) whose base is shaken by white noise, capturing the tuning of the two frequencies, their interaction through the mass ratio, and nonclassical damping. The seven inputs, two masses, two stiffnesses, two damping ratios and the noise intensity, have the independent lognormal distributions of the reliability benchmark of Der Kiureghian and De Stefano, and the active subspaces, with respect to the logarithms of the inputs, come from 1000 samples with exact gradients. The RMS deformation of the equipment spring has a two-dimensional active subspace (λ₁/λ₂ = 12.1, λ₂/λ₃ = 29.4): the first direction is close to the detuning ln(ω_p/ω_s) (cosine 0.97) and the second is mainly the two damping ratios, so the sufficient summary plot recovers the tuning resonance of the paper's Fig. 5. A cubic in the two active variables has leave-one-out R² 0.9116, with its largest misfits at the sharp resonance peak. The benchmark's limit state G = F_s − 3k_s·RMS has weaker gaps (λ₁/λ₂ = 4.44); a cubic in two active variables has leave-one-out R² 0.9376.

### [Undamped_Oscillator_Limit_State](Undamped_Oscillator_Limit_State/)

Rajashekhar and Ellingwood test adaptive response-surface methods for the failure probability of an oscillator with a yielding spring under a load pulse, with the limit state g = 3r − |z_max|. Its six lognormal inputs are the mass, a linear spring, the yielding spring, its yield displacement, the pulse height and the pulse duration; the notebook solves the oscillator exactly, with exact gradients, at 1000 Monte Carlo samples in standardized log inputs. One direction carries 98.44 % of the gradient variance (λ₁/λ₂ = 65.4) and two carry 99.94 % (λ₂/λ₃ = 26.3): the pulse load and its duration lower the safety margin and the yield displacement raises it, while the second direction separates the threshold 3r from the displacement. A quadratic in two active variables has leave-one-out R² 0.99933 and gives the model's failure probability to within 0.6 %. By Monte Carlo that probability is 3.829×10⁻², 4.2 % below the paper's importance-sampling value.
