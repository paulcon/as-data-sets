# Biomedical and cardiovascular

Biomedical, physiological and cardiovascular models.

Each data set below has a short summary of the science: what the model computes, what varies, and what the active subspace shows. The numbers come from the data set's notebook, which has the details and the comparison with the paper. The [top-level README](../README.md#data-sets) has the paper links and the kind of each data set (stored data or Python model).

### [Atrial_Cell_Models_Courtemanche_Maleckar](Atrial_Cell_Models_Courtemanche_Maleckar/)

Coveney and Clayton ran two models of the human atrial action potential, Courtemanche (20 inputs) and Maleckar (22 inputs), on Latin hypercube designs that scale maximum conductances, pump and exchanger rates, Ca²⁺-handling constants, capacitance and extracellular concentrations by ±10–50 %, and recorded 11 action-potential and calcium biomarkers. A second stage repeated the study with 200 runs over the 9 (Courtemanche) or 7 (Maleckar) most influential inputs plus the diastolic interval. The runs give no gradients, so the notebook estimates the subspaces of all 11 biomarkers from a global quadratic fit over the second-stage runs: by a leave-one-out rule, one active variable suffices in 7 of the 22 biomarker × model cases, two in 13 and three in 2. APD₉₀ is one-dimensional for Maleckar (λ₁/λ₂ = 31.2, leave-one-out R² 0.959) and two-dimensional for Courtemanche (λ₁/λ₂ = 7.5; leave-one-out R² 0.820 with one variable, 0.915 with two); in both models it lengthens with the inward currents and shortens with the outward currents, with G_Kr and I_NaK,max important in Courtemanche and G_Kur and C_m in Maleckar. The first-stage designs (about 290 runs for 20 or 22 inputs) support only the direction of a global linear fit, and the Sobol' indices of the fitted models give the paper's top input for 8 of 11 Courtemanche and 10 of 11 Maleckar biomarkers.

### [CiPA_Ventricular_Proarrhythmia_Metrics](CiPA_Ventricular_Proarrhythmia_Metrics/)

The CiPAORdv1.0 model of a human ventricular endocardial myocyte, paced for 1000 beats at a cycle length of 2000 ms, was run for 224 virtual drugs drawn from the nine-input box of Parikh et al.: six percent channel blocks and three hERG drug-binding parameters. The outputs are the paper's eight features: qNet (the integral of six ionic currents over the beat), the action-potential durations APD90 and APD50, the peak potential, and the diastolic level, peak and durations (CaTD50, CaTD90) of the calcium transient. Each run is 1000 beats of a stiff ODE, so there are no gradients and the subspaces come from a global quadratic fit. Seven of the eight features have a one-dimensional active subspace, with λ₁/λ₂ from 8.3 (CaTD90) to 46.1 (peakCa), 24.3 for qNet and 14.1 for APD90, while CaTD50 has a two-dimensional one (λ₂/λ₃ = 12.7 against λ₁/λ₂ = 4.9). A cubic in the active variables has leave-one-out R² 0.972 for qNet and 0.951 for APD90 (one variable) and 0.956 for CaTD50 (two), and the Sobol' indices of a quadratic fit match the paper's qNet shares (bINaL 0.39, sbIKr 0.26, bICaL 0.15, bINa 0.13 against 40, 26, 16 and 13 %).

### [Cole_Cole_Cell_Monolayer_Impedance](Cole_Cole_Cell_Monolayer_Impedance/)

Zhuang et al. describe the electrical impedance of a cell monolayer by a Cole–Cole model with four uncertain parameters: the resistances R₀ and R∞, the dispersion factor α and the time constant τ. The notebook gives them lognormal distributions with the means and standard deviations of the paper, keeps only the physical range α < 1, and computes active subspaces of the real and imaginary parts of the impedance at 31 frequencies between 1 and 10⁷ rad/s from 1022 samples with exact gradients. The structure depends strongly on frequency. Im Z at its peak (ω = 2×10⁵) is nearly one-dimensional (λ₁/λ₂ = 138, with R₀ and α in equal parts; a cubic in y₁ has leave-one-out R² 0.9894), and the size of this gap is set by the upper tail of α. Re Z below 10³ is R₀ alone, while Re Z at 2×10⁶, the paper's boundary between low and high frequencies, has a weaker gap (λ₁/λ₂ = 16) along a direction that mixes α, R∞ and R₀. Monte Carlo under the paper's own Gaussian inputs reproduces its mean impedance curves but not its finding that τ is negligible: the first-order Sobol' index of τ reaches 0.20.

### [Four_Chamber_Heart_Electromechanics](Four_Chamber_Heart_Electromechanics/)

Strocchi et al. ran a patient-specific four-chamber heart electromechanics simulator (CARP coupled to the CircAdapt circulation, about 3,250 core-hours per run) at 407 points in a 45-parameter space, 30 of them confined to a history-matched region, and recorded 20 pressure-volume features. With 407 runs, far fewer than the 1081 coefficients of a full quadratic, and no gradients, the notebook uses a global linear fit, which gives one direction per output. A linear function of the inputs predicts every feature with leave-one-out R² 0.731–0.963, and a polynomial in the one active variable does no better (0.938 for the left-ventricular peak pressure, whose linear fit gets 0.711 of its variance from the systemic resistance), so each feature varies mainly along one direction of the sampled region. How much it varies in other directions is not identified by 407 runs, except that the two end-diastolic pressures have a stable second direction, the atrioventricular delay. A second study on the ToR-ORd ventricular cell model (29 inputs, 2175 runs, global quadratic fit) finds one-dimensional subspaces for all four calcium-transient features (λ₁/λ₂ = 14.4–50.7).

### [Hodgkin_Huxley_Action_Potential](Hodgkin_Huxley_Action_Potential/)

The Hodgkin–Huxley model describes the membrane potential of a nerve axon through its sodium, potassium and leak currents. Torres Valderrama et al. propagate uncertainty in its 11 inputs (four initial conditions, three conductances, three Nernst potentials and the membrane capacitance, each uniform with 20 % variability) and find with time-averaged Sobol' indices that g_K and E_Na carry most of the variance. The notebook computes exact gradients of smooth scalar outputs by forward sensitivity equations at 1000 samples. After a first spike the potential oscillates with decreasing amplitude, and its second, smaller peak has a one-dimensional active subspace (λ₁/λ₂ = 114) dominated by E_Na and g_Na, and a linear function of the active variable has leave-one-out R² 0.9865. The time of that peak needs two directions (λ₁/λ₂ = 4.53, λ₂/λ₃ = 25.3; leave-one-out R² 0.9333 with one, 0.9852 with two), the first mostly potassium and capacitance, the second mostly sodium. Averaged over the paper's time window, the gradients single out the same two inputs as the paper, g_K and E_Na, and the same five negligible ones.

### [Luo_Rudy_Cardiac_Action_Potential](Luo_Rudy_Cardiac_Action_Potential/)

Chang, Strong and Clayton ran the Luo–Rudy 1991 ventricular myocyte model at 200 Latin hypercube samples of its six maximum ionic conductances, each varied by ±25 %, and recorded eight scalar features of the action potential and its restitution, with 20 separate test runs. Without gradients, the subspaces come from a global quadratic fit, with a linear fit as a cross-check. The six action-potential features each have a one-dimensional active subspace, with λ₁/λ₂ from 77.9 (APD₅₀) to 4689 (maximum upstroke velocity), and a quadratic in the one active variable predicts the test runs with R² between 0.9903 and 0.9999. The upstroke velocity is essentially a function of G_Na alone, the resting potential is set by G_K1 and G_b, and APD₉₀ (λ₁/λ₂ = 89.4) grows with the inward calcium conductance G_si and shrinks with G_K and G_b. The two restitution features, the maximum restitution slope and the minimum diastolic interval, have weaker structure (λ₁/λ₂ = 10.1 and 6.9), and even a full quadratic fits them poorly.

### [Pulmonary_Pulse_Wave_Network](Pulmonary_Pulse_Wave_Network/)

Colebank's one-dimensional model of the pulmonary arteries computes blood pressure and flow waves in a vessel network whose outlets end in Windkessel models. Its nine inputs are three wall-stiffness coefficients and a proximal resistance, distal resistance and compliance at each of the two outlets, and new runs at 160 Latin hypercube points, with finite-difference gradients, give the systolic, diastolic and mean pressure at the midpoint of the main pulmonary artery. The pulse pressure (systolic minus diastolic) has a one-dimensional active subspace (λ₁/λ₂ = 9.4) led by the wall-stiffness offset k₃, with smaller contributions from the proximal resistances and the compliances. The systolic pressure is close to one-dimensional (λ₁/λ₂ = 6.6) along a direction that mixes k₃ with all four resistances; a quadratic has leave-one-out R² 0.9146 in one active variable and 0.9522 in two. The mean pressure is a control case: it equals the mean inflow times the parallel outlet resistance, so its subspace is two-dimensional to within 10⁻⁶ of λ₁ and carries no information about the walls or the compliances.

### [Pyloric_Circuit_Energy_Consumption](Pyloric_Circuit_Energy_Consumption/)

Deistler, Macke and Gonçalves ask how the 31 membrane and synaptic conductances of a three-neuron model of the crab pyloric network set its metabolic cost, and find that for circuits matching experimental data the energy has one dominant active direction led by the Na, CaT and CaS conductances. The notebook runs their simulator at 1100 Monte Carlo samples of the whole prior box, where only 7 circuits (0.64 %) are pyloric-like, and takes the circuit energy per second as the output. Without gradients, and because the unregularized quadratic estimator cannot be told apart from noise after the first eigenvalue, the subspace comes from a ridge-regularized quadratic fit (leave-one-out R² 0.531). One direction dominates (λ₁/λ₂ = 4.81, stable under the bootstrap), with the paper's signs for the Na, CaT, CaS and A conductances but led by the AB/PD neuron's conductances over the whole box; on the 277 runs in which all three neurons spike, the linear-fit direction is led by PY's conductances, as in the paper. A cubic in the active variable explains only about half of the energy (leave-one-out R² 0.527).

### [Rabbit_Ventricular_Action_Potential](Rabbit_Ventricular_Action_Potential/)

Yang et al. scale the 15 ionic currents of the Shannon et al. rabbit ventricular myocyte model by factors p_i, pace the cell for 1000 beats, and rank the factors by Sobol' indices of six action potential biomarkers. The notebook's data are runs of the same model and protocol at 320 scrambled Sobol' points in their parameter box, of which 288 give a normal 1:1 response, and the subspaces come from a global quadratic fit to the values. Every biomarker has one dominant direction, with moderate gaps (λ₁/λ₂ = 5.6 for the action potential duration A₉₀ and 16.3 for the resting potential), and a quadratic in two active variables has leave-one-out R² between 0.875 and 0.922 for five of the six biomarkers. The most important input is the Na-K pump factor p_NaK, which the paper ranks seventh, and the runs do not reproduce the biomarker distributions of the authors' stored runs (median A₉₀ 88.6 ms against 144.2 ms); the cause is not known. Varying p_NaK alone, a weak pump gives long action potentials early in the beat train and the shortest ones at beat 1000, after Na loading; at beat 500, where the cells are less Na-loaded, A₉₀ is led by p_Clb, p_K1 and p_Kr, close to the paper's ordering.

### [Tumour_Competition_ODE](Tumour_Competition_ODE/)

Zou and Lewis model the volumes of two competing cell types, Type S and Type R, in a tumour spheroid by a competitive Lotka–Volterra system with six parameters: two growth rates, two carrying capacities and two interaction strengths. They compare global sensitivity rankings with rankings computed on small cells of the parameter cube and measure how far each cell's active subspace is from the global one. The notebook reimplements the model in Python with exact forward-sensitivity gradients at 102,400 points and reproduces the paper's normalized activity scores to within 0.0025 and its distribution of local–global subspace distances (mean 0.7305 against 0.7334). The global active subspace of the total tumour burden over eight weeks is four-dimensional (λ₄/λ₅ = 8.97, the only large eigenvalue ratio; a quadratic in four active variables has leave-one-out R² 0.917). The burden is not low-dimensional in the one- or two-variable sense: what the subspace removes is the two interaction strengths, which span most of the two inactive directions.
