# Stagnation_Temperature_Probe_Design: data

Kind: **stored data**. The values come from ANSYS Fluent RANS simulations of 128 variants of a single-shrouded Kiel stagnation-temperature probe, run by Scillitoe, Ubald, Seshadri and Shahpar for "Design Space Exploration of Stagnation Temperature Probes via Dimension Reduction" (Proc. ASME Turbo Expo 2020, GT2020-16277). The authors published them, with inputs normalized and outputs standardized, as `probes/probes.npz` in the [equadratures/data-sets](https://github.com/equadratures/data-sets) repository. `convert_data.py` copied the archive's arrays into CSV files losslessly and added one derived file, the inputs in physical units. Nothing was rerun, refitted, rescaled or corrected, and no rows were dropped. The original archive (10,932 bytes) is not shipped. Its URL and SHA-256 are below, and `convert_data.py --check` rebuilds the CSVs from it.

The CSVs are plain comma-separated numbers after two `#` lines (a description with the credit, and the column names with units), so `np.loadtxt(path, delimiter=',')` reads them. Every value is written as the shortest decimal that reads back as the same double. Row order is the archive's (run order is not documented).

| `X` | (128, 7) | float64 | "the 7 non-dimensionalised design parameters of each probe" |

Two statements in the archive README are wrong: it gives the outputs the shape (120, 1) (they are (128, 1)), and it says the data were obtained "with the SU2 CFD solver" (the paper, Sec. 3.2, says ANSYS Fluent; we follow the paper).

## Model

Content of the paper card. Page, equation, table and figure numbers refer to the published paper: A. D. Scillitoe, B. Ubald, P. Seshadri and S. Shahpar, "Design Space Exploration of Stagnation Temperature Probes via Dimension Reduction", Proc. ASME Turbo Expo 2020: Turbomachinery Technical Conference and Exposition, Volume 2C: Turbomachinery, V02CT35A057, 2020, doi:10.1115/GT2020-16277. It was read from the ASME Digital Collection PDF (the staged file was named `_oa`, but its text is the publisher's version, paginated V02CT35A057-1 to -12).

- **Simulator** (Secs. 3–3.2, pp. 3–5). A real single-shrouded Kiel temperature probe used in engine tests (Fig. 4; studied in detail in the paper's ref. [2]): Inconel 600 sheath, NiSil/NiCroSil wires (properties averaged so that a symmetry plane can be used), MgO insulation, stainless steel support with alumina insulation. A quarter of the probe is meshed (symmetry planes in y and z) with a structured multi-block mesh of about 4 million nodes "required to achieve satisfactory mesh independence", solids included with conformal interfaces. The 128 meshes are made by RBF morphing of the baseline mesh (Wendland C0 kernel, the authors' parallel C++ tool), applying the seven deformations one after another, about 4,000 CPU hours on the Cambridge CSD3 cluster. ANSYS Fluent solves the compressible steady RANS equations with the realizable k–ε model with streamline-curvature and Kato–Launder stagnation corrections (chosen in the paper's ref. [25]), fully coupled with conduction in the solids. Inflow turbulence intensity 0.5 %, viscosity ratio 5; outlet static pressure 101,325 Pa; inlet 5D upstream and outlet 25D downstream of the probe; the stem and its solids are extended about 11D downstream instead of modelling the wires to the reference junction; no radiation. Each design is run at the six inlet conditions of Table 2 (p. 5):

  | M | P0,in (Pa) | T0,in (K) |
  |---|---|---|
  | 0.3 | 107,850 | 305 |
  | 0.4 | 113,136 | 310 |
  | 0.5 | 120,196 | 315 |
  | 0.6 | 129,241 | 322 |
  | 0.7 | 140,553 | 329 |
  | 0.8 | 154,459 | 338 |

  so that the inlet static temperature stays 300 K: 768 simulations, about 20,000 CPU hours on 16–24 cores per case.
- **Inputs** (m = 7; Table 1, p. 3). Deltas or scale factors relative to the proprietary baseline x0 ("the absolute dimensions are proprietary"). The paper normalizes them by x_j = (2x̂_j − x_U − x_L)/(x_U − x_L) (Eq. 4) and samples them uniformly (p. 4).

  | j | name in `inputs.csv` | parameter (Table 1) | units | x_L | x0 | x_U | archive column | source |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | 1 | `hole_ellipse` | ellipsoidal (bleed) hole, scale | − | 0.50 | 1.00 | 1.25 | 7 | Table 1, p. 3 |
  | 2 | `hole_move` | move hole, translate | mm | −0.3 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 6 | Table 1, p. 3 |
  | 3 | `hole_angle` | angle hole, translate inner opening | mm | 0 | 0.0 | 1.0 | 5, negated | Table 1, p. 3; footnote 3, p. 6 |
  | 4 | `kiel_le` | Kiel leading edge, translate | mm | −2.0 | 0.0 | 2.0 | 4 | Table 1, p. 3 |
  | 5 | `kiel_outer` | Kiel outer diameter, scale | − | 0.90 | 1.00 | 1.25 | 3 | Table 1, p. 3 |
  | 6 | `kiel_inner` | Kiel inner diameter, scale | − | 0.75 | 1.00 | 1.10 | 2 | Table 1, p. 3 |
  | 7 | `hole_diameter` | bleed hole diameter, scale | − | 0.50 | 1.00 | 1.50 | 1 | Table 1, p. 3 |

  Footnote 3 (p. 6): the hole angle is "a translation of the inner bleed hole opening to the fore, so decreasing this parameter leads to less angled bleed holes". The baseline lies on the boundary in two parameters (hole move at x_U = 0 mm, hole angle at x_L = 0 mm); normalized, x0 = (0.333, 1, −1, 0, −0.429, 0.429, 0).
- **Column map (archive → Table 1).** The archive does not name its columns. Archive column j holds Table 1 parameter 8 − j, and archive column 5 (the hole angle) has the opposite sign to the paper's normalized coordinate. This map is **confirmed** by the authors' own fitted subspaces, not just inferred. The evidence:
  1. **The authors' fitted subspaces.** [ascillitoe/probe-subspaces](https://github.com/ascillitoe/probe-subspaces) at commit `072a0b0ea992afb9cbdf1d5862adfb1fe8f7a452` ships `Example_Notebooks/Yp_subspace.joblib` (SHA-256 `a5e8930d…1f453c5f`) and `Example_Notebooks/Rr_subspace.joblib` (SHA-256 `92c777f3…f25c3ca4`), the equadratures `Subspaces` objects (variable projection) that `dim_red.py` loads to draw Figs. 7, 10 and 11. They are uncompressed pickles. **We did not unpickle them** (unpickling runs code from the file); we read the float64 arrays from the raw bytes. In each file the `sample_points` array starts at byte 270 and equals the archive's `X` exactly (`np.array_equal`), and the last 392 bytes before the closing `ub.` are the 7 × 7 matrix `_subspace` (‖WᵀW − I‖ = 1.7 × 10⁻¹⁶ and 3.1 × 10⁻¹⁶). In archive order the loss direction is U_Y = [0.169, 0.0158, 0.9765, −0.018, 0.1227, 0.0123, 0.0452]. Under the map above it becomes [0.0452, 0.0123, −0.1227, −0.0180, 0.9765, 0.0158, 0.1690], which is Eq. 12 to its printed digits: cosine 0.99997, largest difference 0.0048 (reversing without the sign change gives 0.9706). Its active variable spans [−1.155, 1.111] over the 128 designs, the range of Fig. 7, with the baseline at −0.262. The recovery subspace, with the code's direction vectors (designs 58 → 112 and 115 → 26, `dim_red.py` lines 240–248) and its `k[-3] *= -1` (lines 294, 319), gives v_a U_R = [0.337, 0.002, −0.109, 0.003, 0.012, −0.228, 0.907] and v_b U_R = [0.002, −0.046, −0.212, 0.020, −0.504, 0.762, 0.343] in Table 1 order. These are the vectors of Fig. 11; our pixel readings of it (`fig11a`, `fig11b` in the notebook) differ from them by at most 0.0082, and the U_R rebuilt from the readings has principal-angle cosines 0.99999 and 0.99993 with the exact one. The authors' code stores the two recovery directions in the opposite order to the paper: the paper's u_R,1 is the code's second column, the paper's v_a = (1, 0) is the code's (−0.0037, 1.0000), and v_b = (0.15, −0.99) is the code's (−0.9884, 0.1519). The notebook uses the paper's order and quotes the exact vectors (6 decimals) as `UY_authors` and `UR_authors`; principal angles are not affected by the order. Our check script read the files in a scratch folder; the files are not shipped here.
  2. **The baseline vector.** Table 1's x0, normalized by Eq. 4 and put in archive order with the sign change, is [0, 0.4286, −0.4286, 0, 1, 1, 0.3333], which is the code's `Xbaseline = [0, 0.428, -0.429, 0, 1, 1, 1/3]` (`dim_red.py` line 28). This fixes the positions of the hole move, Kiel inner, Kiel outer and ellipse columns and the hole-angle sign (x0 = x_L = 0 mm); Eq. 12 (0.17 against −0.02) then separates the hole diameter from the Kiel leading edge.
  3. **The labels.** The authors' companion script `probe-subspaces/Example_Notebooks/dim_red.py` labels the columns `['Hole ⌀', 'Kiel ⌀inner', 'Kiel ⌀outer', 'Kiel lip fwd/back', 'Hole angle', 'Hole fwd/back', 'Hole ellipse']` (line 38), Table 1 reversed, and before plotting each Fig. 11 vector does `k[-3] *= -1 # Reverse hole angle as updated definition for paper` (lines 294, 319). Its baseline vector in archive order, `Xbaseline = [0, 0.428, -0.429, 0, 1, 1, 1/3]`, has the hole angle at +1, which is Table 1's x_L = 0 mm (normalized −1) after the sign change. (We read this code for its labels, comments and constants only; it is not copied here.)
  4. **The notebook's own estimates.** With this map, the notebook's loss direction matches Eq. 12 with cosine 0.9996 (0.9695 with the archive's sign), the recovery subspace matches the one reconstructed from Fig. 11 with principal-angle cosines 0.9972 and 0.9810 (0.9959 and 0.8802 with the archive's sign), and the baseline falls at u_Y = −0.27, where Fig. 7 draws it (−0.26 by pixel measurement); with the archive's sign the least-squares direction puts it at −0.51. Against the authors' fitted subspaces, the notebook's w1 has cosine 0.99965 with U_Y (its OLS direction 1.00000), and its span(w1, w2) has principal-angle cosines 0.9974 and 0.9816 with U_R.

  `inputs.csv` = `to_physical(to_paper_normalized(X))`, i.e. x̂ = x_L + (x + 1)(x_U − x_L)/2 after the column map. The map is reversible: `convert_data.py` checks that Eq. 4 applied to `inputs.csv`, followed by the inverse column map, gives the archive's `X` to 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶.
- **Quantities of interest** (Sec. 2, pp. 2–3). Y_p = 100 (P0,in − P0,out)/(P0,in − P_out) in % (Eq. 1), outlet values mass-averaged; O_Yp = mean of Y_p over M ∈ {0.3, …, 0.8} (Eq. 2), to be minimized. R_r = T_m/T_0 (measured over true stagnation temperature); O_Rr = |∂R_r/∂M| (Eq. 3), the slope of an ordinary least-squares line through R_r at the six Mach numbers, to be minimized. Sec. 6 uses R_r at M = 0.8. **The archive stores each output standardized to [−1, 1]** ("for commercial privacy reasons"), and each spans exactly [−1, 1], as a min–max scaling over the 128 runs would give. The scaling constants are not published, so the outputs are kept standardized; physical values cannot be recovered from the archive.
- **Output scale read from the paper (for comparison only).** Fig. 3(b) (p. 3) plots O_Rr against O_Yp for all 128 designs in physical units. We rendered p. 3 at 300 dpi, located the 128 marker centroids (128 blobs found) and calibrated the axes from the tick marks, then matched the standardized (y1, y2) pairs to the markers (assignment by the Hungarian method, starting from a min–max map to the marker extremes). 119 of 128 pairs match within 1 % of the axis span (median 0.14 %). A least-squares fit on those pairs gives O_Yp ≈ 0.118 + 0.0393·y1 (%) and O_Rr ≈ 0.00488 + 0.00302·y2, i.e. O_Yp from about 0.079 to 0.157 % and O_Rr from about 0.00186 to 0.0079. We give three significant figures: the residual s.d. of the fits (1.3 × 10⁻⁴ % and 9.9 × 10⁻⁶) measures only how consistently the markers line up, not the error of calibrating the axes from the tick marks, so more digits would be false precision. A decreasing map for y2 does not fit (median mismatch 24 % of the axis span), so `y2` increases with O_Rr; and the text's "values of up to O_Rr = 0.0079" (p. 3) agrees with the upper end. The notebook uses these constants only to compare its baseline loss with Fig. 7 (0.109 % against 0.110 %). They are figure readings, not data, and were not used to convert any file.
- **Physical meaning.** All 128 inputs lie inside the Table 1 box. The outputs are standardized, so no physical bound can be checked; all values are finite, and no run is flagged as failed. No value is outside its meaning, and every row is kept.
- **Gradients.** None. The paper fits polynomial ridge approximations by variable projection (Hokanson and Constantine, SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 40(3):A1566–A1589, 2018, doi:10.1137/17M1117690) with Effective Quadratures. The notebook uses the global quadratic estimator (QPHD; 128 runs for 36 coefficients, at least the 72 the sample-size rule asks), with least squares and local linear gradients as checks.
- **Sampling design in the paper.** "Optimal Latin hypercube sampling (LHS) with N = 128" (p. 4), uniform between x_L and x_U; a footnote says Monte Carlo or other heuristics would do. The archive's design is a Latin hypercube on the 128 levels −1 + 2j/127 (every column takes each level exactly once, to 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁵), so the bounds themselves are sampled. The optimality criterion and seed are not reported. The baseline is not among the 128 designs. The extra designs of Sec. 5 (five at each of six points, Fig. 15) are not in the archive.
- **Cost per run.** About 4,000 CPU hours for all 128 meshes and about 20,000 CPU hours for the 768 CFD runs, i.e. about 30 + 160 CPU hours per design (six Mach numbers).
- **Published results.** Fig. 6: R² of variable-projection ridges against (m, k); m = 1, k = 1 gives R² = 0.992 for O_Yp, and m = 2, k = 3 gives 0.941 for O_Rr (p. 6). Eq. 12: U_Y = [0.05, 0.01, −0.12, −0.02, 0.98, 0.02, 0.17]. Fig. 7: the O_Yp sufficient summary plot (physical units; active variable range [−1.155, 1.111] and baseline u_Y = −0.262, computed from the authors' fitted U_Y; −0.26 and O_Yp ≈ 0.110 % by pixel reading. An earlier pixel reading of the range, [−1.20, 1.11], misread the lower end). Figs. 9–10: the O_Rr surface and contours on (u_R,1, u_R,2), with designs A, B, C on a line at u_R,2 ≈ 0.54 and B near the minimum along it (u_R,1 ≈ 0.15), and the baseline at about (0.13, −0.71) (pixel reading; (0.118, −0.699) from the authors' fitted U_R, whose active variables span [−1.36, 1.12] × [−1.20, 1.51]). Fig. 11: the vectors v_a U_R and v_b U_R, v_a = (1, 0), v_b = (0.15, −0.99), read by pixel measurement in the notebook, within 0.0082 of the exact vectors from the authors' fitted U_R (point 1 above). Sec. 6: R_r(0.8) ridge with m = 2, k = 3, R² = 0.947.

## Regenerating

```bash
python convert_data.py --npz /path/to/probes.npz           # writes the three CSVs (0.26 s)
python convert_data.py --npz /path/to/probes.npz --check   # rebuilds them in memory and compares
```

The script refuses an archive whose SHA-256 differs from the one above. It checks the archive's keys and each array's shape and dtype, that `inputs_normalized.csv` and `outputs.csv` read back with `np.loadtxt` equal to the archive bit for bit (compared as uint64), that the physical inputs lie in the Table 1 box, and that they map back to the archive's `X` within 10⁻¹⁴ (the maximum is 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶). The shipped files were written on 2026-10-31 with numpy 2.5.4 and Python 3.13.18, and `--check` reports all three identical. There is no randomness. The notebook runs in about 15–25 s (25.3 s in the final run, on a shared 2-CPU container). The CFD cannot be rerun: the probe geometry is proprietary (Rolls-Royce), the mesh-morphing tool is the authors' own, and ANSYS Fluent is commercial. The EMA notes kept outside the repository say what a rerun would need.

## Accuracy

- **Conversion:** exact for the two stored files (bit for bit); the physical inputs are exact to rounding (2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ in normalized units).
- **Outputs:** the paper reports mesh independence at about 4 million nodes and a turbulence-model choice validated in its ref. [25]. It gives no iterative convergence tolerances. It notes that the simplifications (no radiation, a support extended into the freestream) make the velocity error more dominant than in the real probe (p. 5), so the "optimal" vent-to-inlet area ratio "should not be taken as a design recommendation" (p. 10). O_Rr is a regression slope over six values and inherits their noise. The notebook's global quadratic leaves LOO R² = 0.9956 (O_Yp), 0.8036 (O_Rr) and 0.8256 (R_r(0.8)); the residuals mix model misfit and CFD noise and cannot be separated here. There are no gradients to check.
- **Uncertainty of the subspace:** the notebook bootstraps the 128 runs 500 times (seed 2020). The first eigenvectors are well determined (median replicate alignment 0.9999 for O_Yp and 0.9958 for O_Rr); the second ones less so (0.9864 and 0.9762, with 4 % of replicates below 0.9).

## How these files relate to the paper

- **The same runs.** The archive's arrays are identical to the authors' companion repository, and with the column map above the notebook reproduces the paper's printed and plotted results: R² = 0.9917 for the linear loss ridge (paper 0.992; the same least-squares fit), the loss direction of Eq. 12 (cosine 0.9996), the recovery subspace of Fig. 11 (principal-angle cosines 0.9972, 0.9810; 0.9974, 0.9816 against the authors' fitted U_R), the low-order R² values of Fig. 6, and the baseline positions in Figs. 7 and 10(a).
- **Differences.** The two-variable cubic fits score R² = 0.928 (O_Rr) and 0.936 (R_r(0.8)) against the paper's 0.941 and 0.947, because the notebook uses the active subspace of the global quadratic, while variable projection optimizes the subspace for the cubic. A cubic refitted on the authors' fitted U_R gives in-sample R² = 0.9423 (O_Rr) and 0.9473 (R_r(0.8)), against the paper's 0.941 and 0.947, which confirms this explanation. The second recovery direction differs from the paper's mainly in the Kiel leading-edge component (−0.19 against about −0.02).
- **Not reproducible from these files:** any value in physical units (the outputs are standardized), the Mach-number curves of Figs. 3(a) and 12, the flow fields (Figs. 8, 13, 16), the new designs of Sec. 5 (Figs. 14–15), and the manufacturing study of Sec. 6 (Fig. 17), which needs the physical tolerances.
