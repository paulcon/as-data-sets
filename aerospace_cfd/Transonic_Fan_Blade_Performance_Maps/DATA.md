# Transonic_Fan_Blade_Performance_Maps: data

Kind: **stored data**. The values come from Rolls-Royce CFD runs (Hydra RANS on PADRAM geometries) of research fan blades. The authors of Seshadri, Shahpar, Constantine, Parks and Adams, "Turbomachinery Active Subspace Performance Maps" (J. Turbomach. 140(4):041003, 2018), published them, "shared with permission from Rolls-Royce plc", as `3Dfan_blades/3Dfan_blades.npz` in the [equadratures/data-sets](https://github.com/equadratures/data-sets) repository. `convert_data.py` copied the archive's nine arrays into three CSV files, losslessly. Nothing was rerun, refitted, rescaled or corrected, and no rows were dropped. The original archive (305,872 bytes) is not shipped. Its URL and SHA-256 are below, and `convert_data.py --check` rebuilds the CSVs from it.

Every value is written as the shortest decimal that reads back as the same double. Row order is the archive's. Blade A's rows are sorted by the first input in the archive, so the order in which the runs were made is lost.

## Model

Content of the paper card. Page, equation and figure numbers refer to the published article: P. Seshadri, S. Shahpar, P. Constantine, G. Parks and M. Adams, "Turbomachinery Active Subspace Performance Maps", J. Turbomach. 140(4):041003, 2018, doi:10.1115/1.4038839 (read from the ASME PDF).

- **Simulator** (Sec. 2.1, pp. 1–2). Rolls-Royce's in-house unstructured solver Hydra solves the steady RANS equations with a five-stage Runge–Kutta scheme, a block Jacobi preconditioner and the Spalart–Allmaras model with wall functions. The domain is a single fan blade passage with a downstream splitter to the engine sector stators (ESS) and outlet guide vanes (OGV). The inlet has a total pressure and total temperature profile, and both exits have non-reflective exit-capacity boundary conditions. The rotor, hub, casing and splitter are viscous walls (rotor and hub in the rotating frame). PADRAM (Rolls-Royce) meshes it with upstream, downstream and periodic H blocks, an O-mesh around the blade and a C-mesh at the splitter: 1.75 × 10⁶ cells after a mesh convergence study, 129 points radially. The blade is a high Mach number research fan whose pressure ratio at 95 % shaft speed is comparable to the cruise working line of modern high bypass ratio fans. Its geometry is proprietary.
- **Inputs** (m = 25; Sec. 2.1, Fig. 2, Eq. 2). PADRAM perturbs five sections, at 0, 25, 50, 75 and 100 % span, and interpolates linearly between them. Each section has five degrees of freedom; none of them changes the thickness. The bounds x̂_L, x̂_U were chosen so that part of the space emulates manufacturing variations and part suits design optimization (Fig. 3 shows the resulting envelope). **Their values are not published**, so the inputs exist only in normalized form: x_j = (2x̂_j − (x̂_U + x̂_L))/(x̂_U − x̂_L) ∈ [−1, 1] (Eq. 2; the paper prints the numerator as 2x̂_j − (x̂_U − x̂_L), which does not map the bounds to ±1, and we use the evident intent), each independent uniform (ρ = 2⁻²⁵, p. 4). x = 0 is the research blade (p. 5).

  | columns | names in the CSV | degree of freedom | units | nominal | range / distribution | source |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | 1–5 | `dihedral_000` … `dihedral_100` | dihedral (axial movement) at 0, 25, 50, 75, 100 % span | normalized (−) | 0 | U[−1, 1] | Sec. 2.1, Eq. 2, Fig. 2; order from Figs. 8(b), 13(b) |
  | 6–10 | `le_recamber_000` … `le_recamber_100` | leading-edge recambering | normalized (−) | 0 | U[−1, 1] | same |
  | 11–15 | `te_recamber_000` … `te_recamber_100` | trailing-edge recambering | normalized (−) | 0 | U[−1, 1] | same |
  | 16–20 | `sweep_000` … `sweep_100` | sweep (tangential displacement) | normalized (−) | 0 | U[−1, 1] | same |
  | 21–25 | `skew_000` … `skew_100` | skew (rotation about the centroidal axis) | normalized (−) | 0 | U[−1, 1] | same |

  **Column order.** The archive does not name its columns. The paper's bar charts (Figs. 8(b), 13(b), 22(b)) order the variables dihedral, LE recamber, TE recamber, sweep, skew, with the 0 % span first in each group (p. 3). Sec. 2.1 lists them in a different order (dihedral, sweep, LE, TE, skew). With the bar-chart order, blade A's first eigenvectors match the paper's bars to |w₁·w₁ᵖᵃᵖᵉʳ| = 0.992 (efficiency) and 0.988 (pressure ratio). With the text order they match to 0.435 and 0.244. So the archive's columns are in the bar-chart order.
- **Quantities of interest** (Sec. 3, p. 3). At cruise (95 % shaft speed, on the working line, with the cruise geometry), the adiabatic efficiency η = 100 (PR^((γ−1)/γ) − 1)/(TR − 1) (Eq. 1), where PR and TR are the total pressure and temperature ratios mass-averaged over the ESS and OGV exits and γ is the ratio of specific heats, and the pressure ratio PR. The archive's `y1` and `y2` are "non-dimensional" versions. Their sample mean is zero to 1e-13 in every blade, so they are deviations from the blade's sample mean. Their scale is not documented. The paper's figures suggest that the archive's values are the paper's divided by roughly 3 (efficiency, in %) and roughly 4 (pressure ratio). For efficiency: Fig. 9(a) has 0.2 % between contour lines (caption, p. 5) and about 15–18 fill levels, so it spans about 2.8–3.6 %, 2.7–3.4 times blade A's range of 1.052; Fig. 6's 150 efficiencies span about 3.7 % (0.5 % scale bar), about 3.5 times; and the first value of Fig. 7(a) is 2.96 times the notebook's √λ₁. For pressure ratio: Fig. 16(a) has 0.005 between contour lines (caption, p. 7) and about 20–21 fill levels, so it spans about 0.095–0.105, 3.5–3.9 times blade A's range of 0.0269; and Fig. 7(b) gives 3.97. These factors are inferences from figure readings of the paper's own (different) design, not stated anywhere; whether the two factors really differ cannot be settled from them. Through them, the notebook's gain along the paper's corridor v (0.143) is 0.42 % and its pressure-ratio drop 0.027–0.034, the same order as the paper's 0.4 % and 0.041 (pp. 7–8). The paper's other quantities, maximum climb flow capacity at 105 % speed (Sec. 3.1, Fig. 4) and efficiency–sensitivity to a manufacturing perturbation (Sec. 5, Figs. 22–24), are not in the archive.
- **Physical meaning.** All 1406 input rows lie in [−0.9999, 0.9998], inside the normalized box. The outputs are centred deviations, so negative values are expected. No value is outside its physical meaning, and no run is flagged as failed (all values are finite).
- **Gradients.** None. The paper estimates the active subspaces from a global least-squares quadratic in all 25 inputs, with 351 coefficients (Eqs. 6–11, p. 4), and from a linear fit for capacity (Eqs. 3–5). Its Eq. 11 prints C = ⅓A² + ccᵀ for f = xᵀAx + cᵀx + d. The gradient of that f is 2Ax + c, so the uniform average is ⁴⁄₃A² + ccᵀ. The notebook uses the latter, which is the library's QPHD estimator, and the paper's Fig. 7 agrees better with it (see below). The notebook also uses local linear gradients and OLS as cross-checks.
- **Sampling design in the paper.** An optimal Latin hypercube of N = 150 (the linear recipe), then 350 more for N = 500 (p. 4). No seed is given. A footnote (p. 3) says Monte Carlo would do as well. **The archive's designs are different** (checked in the notebook). No blade is a Latin hypercube: in some column a stratum of width 1/M holds 8 points (A) or 7 (B, C), where a Latin hypercube has one. Blade A is a mixture: 458 designs fill [−1, 1]²⁵ (largest |x_j| of each between 0.76 and 1) and 90 lie in [−0.4, 0.4]²⁵ (largest |x_j| between 0.33 and 0.40), so its marginals are peaked (kurtosis 1.94–2.18). Blades B and C share 307 designs, and B contains x = 0. No blade has duplicate rows, and blade A shares no design with B or C.
- **Cost per run.** Not reported. Each design needs a PADRAM mesh of 1.75 × 10⁶ cells and a Hydra solve on the working line.
- **Published results** (from figures; no tables). Fig. 7: the "eigenvalues" for efficiency (a) and pressure ratio (b), read by pixel measurement as 0.985, 0.245, 0.158, … 0.0057 and 0.0357, 0.00687, 0.00147, … 6.58e-5. Figs. 8(b) and 13(b): the first two eigenvectors, read to about ±0.01. The notebook holds all four sets of values. Fig. 9(a): the efficiency map, with a high-efficiency corridor along v = (0.4, −1.6) from the research blade at (0, 0). Fig. 16: pressure-ratio maps, (a) piecewise linear, (b) a quadratic. Fig. 17: the efficiency zonotope, about [−2.9, 2.9] × [−3.2, 3.2]. Fig. 20(b): pressure ratio on the efficiency map. The paper uses n = 2 for both quantities.

## Which blade is the paper's

The archive's README says the data are "used in" the 2018 paper and in Seshadri, Yuchi, Parks and Shahpar, "Supporting multi-point fan design with dimension reduction", Aeronaut. J. 124(1279):1371–1398, 2020, doi:10.1017/aer.2020.50. It does not say which blade is the 2018 research blade. The notebook compares all three blades with the paper and finds that **blade A** matches best:

- The shape of the spectrum, as the root-mean-square log₁₀ difference of √(λ_k/λ₁) from Fig. 7, is 0.157 (efficiency) and 0.072 (pressure ratio) for A, against 0.265 and 0.348 for C. Blade B cannot be compared, because it has too few runs for the quadratic.
- The efficiency eigenvectors give |w₁·w₁ᵖᵃᵖᵉʳ| = 0.992 and |w₂·w₂ᵖᵃᵖᵉʳ| = 0.907 for A, against 0.981 and 0.715 for C.
- For B, OLS gives 0.959 for efficiency and 0.825 for pressure ratio, and B's efficiency is almost a ridge function of one direction (local linear λ₁/λ₂ = 3623.5). That is unlike the paper's curved efficiency ridge (Fig. 8(a)).
- The output scales implied by the paper's figures (roughly 3 for efficiency, 4 for pressure ratio; see "Quantities of interest") are similar for Fig. 7, which matches blade A's spectrum, and for the contour maps of Figs. 9(a) and 16(a). (The 3-D plot of Fig. 13(a) cannot be used for a range: its vertical extent mixes all three axes.)

Two observations cut the other way. Blade C's pressure-ratio subspace matches the paper as well as A's does (0.993 and 0.978). And the paper's Fig. 6 (p. 4) marks the research blade as a run at wᵀx = 0, while blade A has no x = 0 row (its closest design, row 304, has |x| = 0.904; blade B contains x = 0). Blade A has 548 designs, not 500, and is not a Latin hypercube, so it is at best a superset or a variant of the paper's design. This is an inference from the data, listed as an open question for the authors.

## Regenerating

```bash
python convert_data.py --npz /path/to/3Dfan_blades.npz           # writes the three CSVs (0.15 s)
python convert_data.py --npz /path/to/3Dfan_blades.npz --check   # rebuilds them in memory and compares
```

The script refuses an archive whose SHA-256 differs from the one above. It checks the archive's keys, each array's shape and dtype, and that each CSV reads back with `np.loadtxt` equal to the source arrays bit for bit (compared as uint64). The shipped files were written on 2026-10-30 with numpy 2.5.4 and Python 3.13.18, and `--check` reports all three identical. There is no randomness. The CFD cannot be rerun outside Rolls-Royce: Hydra and PADRAM are proprietary, and the blade geometry and design bounds are not public. The EMA notes kept outside the repository say what a rerun would need.

## Accuracy

- **Conversion:** exact (bit for bit, as above).
- **Outputs:** the paper reports a mesh convergence study (1.75 × 10⁶ cells, Sec. 2.1) and earlier CFD–experiment validations of PADRAM–Hydra for fans and compressors (its refs. [8, 11]). No convergence tolerance is given. The paper's Sec. 4.3 checks that designs with the same efficiency active coordinates but different full coordinates differ in CFD efficiency by at most 0.18 % (0.015 % for pressure ratio, 0.2 % for capacity). That number measures the inactive-subspace variation, not solver error. The notebook's quadratic fits leave LOO residuals that give LOO R² = 0.9423 (efficiency) and 0.9937 (pressure ratio). These residuals are mostly misfit of the quadratic to the CFD, not CFD noise. There are no gradients to check.
- **Uncertainty of the subspace:** the library's pairs bootstrap is not usable here (a replicate of 548 rows has about 347 distinct rows for 351 coefficients; 135 of 200 replicate fits are rank-deficient and its bands exclude the estimates), so the notebook uses a wild (Rademacher, leverage-corrected) residual bootstrap of the quadratic with the design held fixed. Its replicate ranges describe sensitivity to perturbations of the size of the residuals; they omit design-to-design variability, and from k = 3 they lie above the estimates (upward bias of small eigenvalues), so they are not confidence intervals there.

## How these files relate to the paper

- **The same kind of runs, probably not exactly the same set.** These are the authors' CFD values as published, not a regeneration. Blade A reproduces the paper's first eigenvectors (0.992, 0.988) and the structure of its maps. It differs from the paper's stated design (548 runs, a mixture of two boxes, not a Latin hypercube), and its active-variable ranges differ from the paper's maps (efficiency y₁ ∈ [−1.80, 1.44] against about [−1.4, 1.55] in Fig. 9(a); pressure ratio y₁ ∈ [−1.35, 1.64] against about [−1.7, 1.4] in Fig. 16(a)).
- **Eigenvalues.** Fig. 7 matches the square roots of the notebook's eigenvalues in shape, not the eigenvalues. The notebook gets λ₁/λ₂ = 18.6 (efficiency) and 36.1 (pressure ratio), and Fig. 7's ratios are 4.02 and 5.20, whose squares are 16.2 and 27.0. The paper's printed Eq. 11 (⅓A²) fits Fig. 7 worse than the correct ⁴⁄₃A² (0.369 against 0.157, and 0.340 against 0.072). We do not know what Fig. 7 plots. Singular values of a factor of C would fit, since the paper says the decomposition was computed "using singular value decomposition".
- **Dimension.** The paper uses n = 2 for both quantities. On the eigenvalues, the gap rule picks n = 1 for both. A second active variable still raises the LOO R² of a quadratic from 0.9470 to 0.9843 (efficiency) and from 0.9807 to 0.9983 (pressure ratio), so the 2-D maps are justified. These LOO values hold the subspace fixed; with it re-estimated in each of 10 folds, the 2-D quadratic scores 0.9769 and 0.9975.
- **Not reproducible from these data:** flow capacity (Figs. 4, 5, 20(a)), efficiency–sensitivity (Figs. 22–24), the extra CFD runs at inverse-mapped designs (Figs. 17(b), 18), and any magnitude in the paper's units.
