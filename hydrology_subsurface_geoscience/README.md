# Hydrology, subsurface and geoscience

Surface and subsurface hydrology, land-surface models, porous media, glaciers and geophysics.

Each data set below has a short summary of the science: what the model computes, what varies, and what the active subspace shows. The numbers come from the data set's notebook, which has the details and the comparison with the paper. The [top-level README](../README.md#data-sets) has the paper links and the kind of each data set (stored data or Python model).

### [Aquifer_Tracer_Advection_Diffusion](Aquifer_Tracer_Advection_Diffusion/)

Jakeman uses this transient advection–diffusion benchmark to demonstrate the PyApprox package: a tracer released by a source in a unit-square aquifer is advected and diffuses after the source is shut off. The log-diffusivity is a Karhunen–Loève expansion (KLE), here with 10 independent standard normal coefficients (the paper's example uses 3), and the output is the tracer mass in a downstream subdomain at the final time, with exact gradients at 500 Monte Carlo samples. The gradients show a three-dimensional active subspace (λ₃/λ₄ = 11.6, λ₂/λ₃ = 8.7, λ₁/λ₂ = 2.7), made up mostly of the four largest KLE modes. A cubic in the three active variables has leave-one-out R² 0.983 (0.953 with two). The notebook computes on a converged Chebyshev mesh of order 21; the benchmark's coarser mesh rotates the two-dimensional subspace by 9.6°, far more than its sampling error.

### [CMF_Soil_Moisture_SPOTPY](CMF_Soil_Moisture_SPOTPY/)

Houska et al. calibrated four van Genuchten–Mualem soil parameters of a one-dimensional CMF soil column (Richards equation) against a year of daily topsoil soil moisture at the Linden FACE grassland site, and deposited every run of their Monte Carlo and Latin hypercube samplers with its RMSE. On the 9,972 valid Monte Carlo runs (draws from the normal prior), a global quadratic fit gives λ₁/λ₂ = 108.0 for the RMSE, with a leading direction dominated by the shape parameter n (+0.79) and the porosity φ (−0.566). The quadratic has leave-one-out R² of only 0.8338, because it cannot follow the steep rise of the RMSE as α → 0: the 36 runs with α < 0.05 1/cm hold 0.0635 of the sum of squares, and a second, almost pure-α direction describes them (leave-one-out R² 0.8913 for a polynomial in one active variable, 0.9609 in two). For the bulk of the prior the RMSE is a V-shaped function of one active variable, the "high equifinality" the paper reports. The Latin hypercube runs, whose uniform box puts 787 runs in the small-α corner, are two-dimensional unless that corner is left out.

### [CO2_Injection_Benchmark_Saturation](CO2_Injection_Benchmark_Saturation/)

The benchmark of Köppel et al. injects CO₂ for 100 days into a radially symmetric saline aquifer, and the output is the CO₂ saturation S(r) along the radius. Three uncertain inputs vary, the injection rate, the degree of the relative permeability curves and the porosity, over 10,000 samples run with the authors' own finite-volume code; these are the runs behind the paper's Monte Carlo reference. For the radius of the CO₂ plume front, local linear fits give a one-dimensional active subspace (λ₁/λ₂ = 78): w₁ is the porosity (0.997) with a small injection-rate part (0.080), and a polynomial in this one variable has leave-one-out R² 0.978. The saturation at r = 100.4 m, where the reference standard deviation peaks, has nearly the same leading direction but jumps as the front passes. This agrees with the paper's statement that the injection rate and the porosity are the main sources of uncertainty and that the relative permeability degree hardly matters (its weight is 0.007), and it adds an ordering: porosity first by far.

### [Earthquake_Rupture_Seismic_Moment](Earthquake_Rupture_Seismic_Moment/)

Daub et al. fit a Gaussian-process emulator to 30 two-dimensional dynamic rupture simulations of a rough fault (the open-source code fdfault) and use history matching to find the initial stresses consistent with an observed earthquake size. The output here is that size, the integrated final slip, over the paper's three uniform stress inputs: the normal stress, the shear-to-normal stress ratio and the ratio of the normal stress components. With 30 runs and no gradients, a global quadratic fit gives a one-dimensional active subspace (λ₁/λ₂ = 43.6, 95 % bootstrap interval 12.1–87.3), with w₁ dominated by the shear-to-normal stress ratio (0.977), a smaller weight of opposite sign on the normal stress (−0.214) and almost none on the normal stress ratio (−0.004). A quadratic in the one active variable has leave-one-out R² 0.979, the same as a full quadratic in all three inputs. The order of the inputs matches the paper's emulator correlation lengths, and w₁ has the same signs and dominant input as the normal to the paper's history-matching curve.

### [Freyberg_MODFLOW_Forecast](Freyberg_MODFLOW_Forecast/)

The Freyberg (1988) synthetic water-table aquifer, rebuilt in MODFLOW-2005 by Hunt, Fienen and White with 70 pilot-point hydraulic conductivities and one recharge rate, forecasts the heads at six pumping wells after a river is lined. The 71 inputs are sampled log-uniformly over the prior box of the calibration (the PEST bounds), with gradients from forward differences in log₁₀ of each input; the analysis keeps the 333 of 500 samples in which no active cell goes dry at the sample or at its difference points. The forecast head at well fr20c14 has a two-dimensional active subspace, recharge first and then the conductivity around the well (λ₁/λ₂ = 17.0, λ₂/λ₃ = 37.5), and a cubic in two active variables has leave-one-out R² 0.9959. The notebook also reruns the release's calibrated model, which reproduces the paper's calibration and forecast errors, and a linear uncertainty analysis at it puts all six true forecasts inside their 95 % intervals, as in the paper.

### [Geological_CO2_Storage_Leakage](Geological_CO2_Storage_Leakage/)

Amrhein et al. estimate how long CO₂ injected into a reservoir takes to leak to the ground surface, and at what rate, with a closed-form one-dimensional Darcy model of the layers above the reservoir. The uncertain inputs are the layer permeabilities and depths, the injection pressure and a viscosity factor, 22 for the Ketzin site (Germany) and 30 for Quest (Canada), with exact gradients at 2000 samples per site. The logarithm of the total leakage time has a dominant direction (λ₁/λ₂ = 5.49 at Ketzin, 6.17 at Quest) and a two-dimensional active subspace that carries 97 % of the gradient variance, built almost entirely from the permeabilities of the two tightest seal layers and the viscosity; a cubic on it has leave-one-out R² 0.9859 at Ketzin. The logarithm of the minimum flow rate (smoothed, because the minimum over layers has a kink) is genuinely two-dimensional (λ₁/λ₂ = 1.44, λ₂/λ₃ = 21.3), the second direction being the difference of the two seal permeabilities across which the minimum switches layers. Total Sobol' indices from the model agree with the paper's sensitivity indices to within 10 percentage points.

### [HBV_SASK_Oldman_Streamflow](HBV_SASK_Oldman_Streamflow/)

HBV-SASK is a daily conceptual hydrologic model with 12 parameters that ships with VARS-TOOL, the sensitivity-analysis toolbox of Razavi et al., set up for the Oldman River at Waldron's Corner, Alberta. The model is computed with forward-sensitivity gradients, and the 12 parameters are sampled uniformly on the paper's box (2000 samples). The main output is the Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE) of simulated daily streamflow over 1982–1996, with the mean flow and the mean annual peak flow as secondary outputs. The NSE has a dominant direction (λ₁/λ₂ = 3.91; a cubic in one active variable has leave-one-out R² 0.906), made mostly of the precipitation multiplier PM, the fast-reservoir fraction FRAC, the melt factor C0 and the field capacity FC. These are the four parameters that lead the Sobol' total-effect indices and the VARS-TOOL tutorial's variance-based ranking for this output.

### [HyMod_Leaf_River_Rainfall_Runoff](HyMod_Leaf_River_Rainfall_Runoff/)

Pianosi et al. illustrate their SAFE toolbox for global sensitivity analysis on HyMod, a five-parameter daily rainfall-runoff model, with the Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE) of simulated against observed Leaf River flow as the output. The notebook uses 2000 Monte Carlo samples with exact (almost-everywhere) gradients over the first 365 days of the record, with a 30-day warm-up and all stores empty at day 1, as in the toolbox's example. On the paper's ranges the NSE has a two-dimensional active subspace, because the 2.1 % of samples with a soil store S_m below 10 mm, where the store degenerates, carry 52.1 % of the gradient mass; with S_m ≥ 10 mm it is one-dimensional (λ₁/λ₂ = 12.81) along a combination of the quick-flow coefficient R_f and the split alfa, consistent with the paper's Elementary Effects ranking. The peak simulated flow has a one-dimensional active subspace along R_f and alfa (λ₁/λ₂ = 18.19; leave-one-out R² 0.952 for a cubic), while the mean simulated flow is not well summarized by one or two active variables. These ratios are specific to that first year: with a one-year spin-up and three other water years the R_f–alfa direction persists (for the peak flow, except in 1959), but the NSE's λ₁/λ₂ on S_m ≥ 10 mm falls to 3.26–5.62 and the peak flow's ranges from 2.02 to 17.06.

### [Kaesbach_Catchment_Subsurface_Flow](Kaesbach_Catchment_Subsurface_Flow/)

Erdal and Cirpka ran 10,050 steady-state simulations of a three-dimensional, variably saturated HydroGeoSphere model of a catchment modelled on the Käsbach (Ammer valley, Germany), with 32 uncertain hydraulic, geological and boundary parameters, and kept the runs that meet five expert "behavioral" targets (4,528 in the stored runs). As in the paper, the active subspaces come from the gradients of a global quadratic fit. The stream discharge at gauge C has λ₁/λ₂ = 4.62 and λ₂/λ₃ = 3.18; w₁ is led by the Muschelkalk conductivity (−0.671), the weathered-Gipskeuper conductivity (+0.582) and the bottom head offset (+0.379), w₂ by the Lettenkeuper conductivity and the fault offset, and a cubic meta-model has NSE 0.672 in two active variables against 0.532 in one, which supports the paper's choice of two. The net outflow across the subsurface boundaries is also two-dimensional (λ₁/λ₂ = 6.60, λ₂/λ₃ = 3.15; NSE 0.753), with a first direction close to that of the discharge (|w₁·w₁′| = 0.898): the two flows are two sides of the same partitioning of recharge. These subspaces describe the behavioral (posterior-like) sample, not the uniform prior box, and the notebook shows how much that matters.

### [Karst_Aquifer_LuKARS_Calibration](Karst_Aquifer_LuKARS_Calibration/)

LuKARS is a lumped model of a karst spring that sums the quickflow of three soil and land-cover units (hydrotopes) and a shared baseflow store. Teixeira Parente et al. calibrated its 21 hydrotope parameters to the daily discharge of the Kerschbaum spring (Austria, 2006–2008), and the output is the Bayesian data misfit with 5 % Gaussian noise, here at 1000 Monte Carlo samples of the paper's normalized calibration parameters with exact gradients. The misfit has a two-dimensional active subspace (λ₁/λ₂ = 1.85, λ₂/λ₃ = 6.73) spanned by the quickflow and recharge coefficients of hydrotopes 1 and 2. A quartic polynomial in the two active variables predicts 5000 independent misfits with R² 0.787. The paper's larger spectrum, with its first gap after λ₁, is largely reproduced by finite differences taken through the 6-decimal rounding of the parameters in the authors' code interface, so it is likely, though not confirmed, a gradient artifact.

### [Kurchatov_Sr90_Groundwater_Migration](Kurchatov_Sr90_Groundwater_Migration/)

Volkova, Iooss and Van Dorpe built a groundwater-flow and Sr-90 transport model of the Kurchatov Institute radwaste site with the code MARTHE and propagated 20 uncertain inputs through 300 runs; the outputs are the predicted 2010 concentrations at the observation wells, 10 of which are public. The paper finds that they depend mainly on the Sr-90 distribution coefficients of the first two layers (kd1, kd2) and on the infiltration rate in the leakage zones (i3). With every input mapped through its own distribution function to independent uniform coordinates, and no gradients, the notebook estimates a one-dimensional active subspace from a linear model. One direction carries the trend at the two wells that a linear model fits best: p104 (led by kd1, +0.903, and i3, −0.365; a cubic has leave-one-out R² 0.937) and log₁₀ p29K (kd2 −0.788, kd1 −0.346, i3 +0.339; leave-one-out R² 0.868). At the other eight wells the cubic explains 13–62 % of the variance, but on the rank scale nearly the same direction explains 0.505 to 0.848 of the variance at six of them, so the poor fits there are largely an effect of the concentration scale.

### [Land_Surface_Energy_Fluxes](Land_Surface_Energy_Fluxes/)

The ParFlow-Common Land Model (PF-CLM) is an integrated hydrologic model that combines groundwater and land-surface processes and computes the latent, sensible and ground heat fluxes at the land surface. Jefferson et al. vary 19 of its land-surface parameters in a single-column domain with meteorological forcing from the Little Washita watershed (Oklahoma), for uniform cover of bare soil, which uses only three of them (roughness length, soil color and water table depth), or of grass, which uses all 19; there are 300 runs for each cover and no gradients. A least-squares linear fit gives one direction per flux, and on it a quadratic has leave-one-out R² from 0.814 to 0.992, tighter for bare soil than for grass and for ground heat than for latent and sensible heat. For bare soil the roughness length has the largest weight for latent and ground heat and soil color for sensible heat; for grass, field capacity, leaf area index, water table depth and wilting point lead the latent heat, near-infrared leaf transmittance the sensible heat, and leaf and stem area index the ground heat. The leaf area index raises latent heat and lowers ground heat, as the paper says. The stored runs are later model runs than those behind the paper's figures, and the notebook prints each difference beside the paper's value.

### [Photosynthesis_Stomatal_Resistance](Photosynthesis_Stomatal_Resistance/)

Land-surface models condense the plant physiology that controls transpiration into one term, the stomatal resistance, which in the Ball–Berry parameterization depends on the rate of photosynthesis. Jefferson et al. vary the 20 photosynthesis and stomatal-resistance parameters of PF-CLM in 300 runs with one year of hourly forcing from Tonzi Ranch, California (September 2002 to August 2003), and study transpiration and the stomatal resistance of the sunlit and the shaded canopy as monthly and hourly averages, 78 outputs in all. Without gradients, each output gets one direction from a least-squares linear fit. That direction describes transpiration well in every slice (R² at least 0.89) and shaded resistance reasonably (0.75 to 0.82), but sunlit resistance only partly in summer (0.46 in July, 0.37–0.44 in the afternoon hours of June–August). The Ball–Berry intercept bp and slope mp and the parameter wj1 carry the largest weights for transpiration and shaded resistance, and sunlit resistance shows the most seasonal variation, with vcmx25, kc25, ocr and qe25 also mattering at some point in the year.

### [PyGEM_Glacier_Mass_Balance](PyGEM_Glacier_Mass_Balance/)

Rounce et al. calibrate three parameters of the Python Glacier Evolution Model (PyGEM), the precipitation factor k_p, the temperature bias T_bias and the degree-day factor of snow f_snow, against one geodetic mass balance per glacier, and show that the three are not identifiable from it. The notebook runs the paper's model on a substitute glacier, Hintereisferner (Ötztal Alps, Austria), with ERA5 forcing, and computes the 2000–2017 mass balance B with complex-step gradients at 1000 samples. Over a box containing the regional prior means of k_p, B has a one-dimensional active subspace (λ₁/λ₂ = 29.6, with 25.8 and 115.2 on a wider and a narrower box), and a cubic in the active variable has leave-one-out R² 0.972. Its direction, more k_p against more T_bias and f_snow, is the trade-off behind the paper's non-identifiability. Freeing the precipitation gradient and the snow/rain threshold adds almost nothing.

### [Raven_Salmon_River_Blended_Model](Raven_Salmon_River_Blended_Model/)

Mai et al. run a Raven hydrologic model of the Salmon River (British Columbia) whose five blended processes mix 13 published process options with weights set by eight uniform numbers, so the model has 43 uniform inputs (35 parameters and 8 weight-generating numbers), and they rank parameters, process options and processes by Sobol' indices of the daily streamflow. From 4096 stored runs without gradients, a global quadratic fit gives the mean simulated flow over 1991–2010 a well-determined leading direction, a combination of the quickflow exponent x₆, the PET correction x₈, the maximum quickflow rate x₅, the rain correction x₃₃ and the quickflow and evaporation weight numbers. How dominant it is depends on the estimator (λ₁/λ₂ = 6.75 from the quadratic, 46.2–54.7 from local linear fits), and about a fifth of the variance lies in directions 2–10, so one active variable is an approximate ridge, not a tight one. Summed over the 12 monthly mean flows, the derivative-based sensitivities include 11 of the paper's 13 most sensitive parameters. The monthly process shares agree with the paper from March to November (potential melt leads in spring, quickflow and evaporation in summer) but not in December–February.

### [Runoff_Permeability_Field](Runoff_Permeability_Field/)

Runoff forms either when the soil is saturated and added water is forced out (Dunnian) or when rain falls faster than the soil can absorb it (Hortonian). Gilbert et al. compute the cumulative runoff volume at the outlet over 48 hours with ParFlow on an idealized "tilted-v" domain in which only the subsurface permeability varies: its logarithm is a Gaussian random field with 100 Karhunen–Loève (KL) coefficients as inputs. Each scenario was run 10,000 times with the same inputs and no gradients, and a least-squares linear fit gives one direction; 31 % of the Hortonian runs produce no runoff and are left out, as in the paper. Dunnian runoff is close to a function of one active variable that is almost entirely the first KL coefficient, so it is sensitive mainly to large-scale heterogeneity; Hortonian runoff has a much looser trend, with large weights on several low-index coefficients and more weight on small-scale changes. A global quadratic fit, beyond the paper, gives nearly the same first direction (λ₁/λ₂ = 19.4 Dunnian, 7.6 Hortonian), and a cubic in that Hortonian active variable has leave-one-out R² 0.7494.

### [Subglacial_Drainage_GlaDS](Subglacial_Drainage_GlaDS/)

Hill et al. ran the GlaDS subglacial drainage model 612 times on a synthetic 100 km × 25 km ice-sheet margin, varying eight parameters (512 training runs on a Sobol' sequence, 100 test runs on a Latin hypercube). The outputs are the channel discharge fraction f_Q, the log sheet transit time log₁₀ T_s and the channel network length L_c, and without gradients the subspaces come from a global quadratic fit. f_Q and log₁₀ T_s each have one dominant direction (λ₁/λ₂ = 10.97 and 12.43; test R² 0.959 and 0.911): f_Q rises with the bed bump aspect ratio and the channel conductivity and falls with the sheet conductivity, and log₁₀ T_s is led by the sheet conductivity. L_c needs two active variables (test R² 0.764 with one, 0.919 with two), and its first direction is dominated by the width of sheet beneath channels. The order of the inputs by activity score agrees with the order of the paper's Sobol' indices (rank correlations 0.97–0.99).
